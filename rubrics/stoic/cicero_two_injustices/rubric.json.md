# Stoic: The two kinds of injustice (Cicero, De Officiis 1.23)

## inflicting wrong

The first of Cicero's two kinds of injustice: actively inflicting wrong, often from passion, fear or avarice.

> There are, on the other hand, two kinds of injustice — **the one, on the part of those who inflict wrong**... For he who, under the influence of anger or some other passion, wrongfully assaults another seems, as it were, to be laying violent hands upon a comrade... those very wrongs which people try to inflict on purpose to injure are often the result of fear... But, for the most part, people are led to wrong-doing in order to secure some personal end; in this vice, avarice is generally the controlling motive.

Cicero, On Duties1.23–24, tr.Miller1913 (Academic following Panaetius)

> Still, I do not mean to find fault with the accumulation of property, provided it hurts nobody, but unjust acquisition of it is always to be avoided.

Cicero, On Duties1.23–25, tr.Miller1913 (Academic following Panaetius)

Limits: Ordinary wealth is not this vice. Cicero names no antonym virtue for this list; none is invented here. Harm in response to provocation is a separate qualifier (1.20).

## not shielding from wrong

Cicero's second injustice: failing, when able, to protect from wrong those one ought to defend.

> ...the other on the part of those who, when they can, do not shield from wrong those upon whom it is being inflicted... **but he who does not prevent or oppose wrong, if he can, is just as guilty of wrong as if he deserted his parents or his friends or his country.**

Cicero, On Duties1.23–24, tr.Miller1913 (Academic following Panaetius)

> ...people either are reluctant to incur enmity or trouble or expense; or through indifference, indolence, or incompetence, or through some preoccupation or self-interest they are so absorbed that they suffer those to be neglected whom it is their duty to protect... For they secure one sort of justice, to be sure, in that they do no positive wrong to anyone, but they fall into the opposite injustice... But in fact it were better that they should assume them of their own accord; for an action intrinsically right is just only on condition that it is voluntary.

Cicero, On Duties1.28, tr.Miller1913 (Academic following Panaetius)

Not inflicting wrong does not, by itself, resist this omission.

Limits: This applies when the agent is able, and to those one ought to protect. Genuine inability is not this vice. The source does not set out degrees of ability.
