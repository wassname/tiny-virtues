# Stoic: Self-respecting, faithful, high-minded (Epictetus, Handbook 24)

## faithful

Trustworthiness and a friend's sound judgements (Handbook 24; Discourses 2.4, 4.13), not religious faith.

> Show me that, if a man unbosoms himself to somebody about his own affairs, he is faithful and respectful. ... But that is not the case; **to be faithful and respectful a man needs judgements of no casual sort.** ... Remember, therefore, in general, that confidences require faithfulness and faithful judgements

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses4.13

> **I listen and keep still, if, to be sure, I happen to be that kind of a person, but he goes out and tells everybody.** ... Man, I did not invite your confidences, did I? You did not tell about your affairs on certain conditions, that you were to hear about mine in return, did you?

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses4.13

> **man is born to fidelity** ... As what am I to treat you, fellow? As a neighbour, as a friend? ... What confidence am I to place in you?

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses2.4

Sharing secrets first is not proof. Guarding information does not oblige disclosure in return. The 2.4 property framing is historical source data, not advice.

## high-minded

Handbook 24 and Discourses 2.8: honour sought only in what is under one's control.

> Let not these reflections oppress you: “I shall live without honour, and be nobody anywhere.” ... It is not your business, is it, to get office, or to be invited to a dinner-party? ... **you ought to be somebody only in those things which are under your control, wherein you are privileged to be a man of the very greatest honour**

Epictetus, Handbook 24, trans. Oldfather

> be abased if dressed in rags or conceited if he has on fine clothes? ... A lofty air, say you? Heaven forbid! **For the Zeus at Olympia does not show a proud look, does he? No, but his gaze is steady**

Epictetus, Discourses 2.8, trans. Oldfather

> “What place, then, shall I have in the State?” says he. **Whatever place you can have, and at the same time maintain the man of fidelity and self-respect that is in you.**

Epictetus, Handbook 24, trans. Oldfather

## self-respecting

Handbook 24, with Discourses 2.8 and 4.9: keeping one's moral choice decent, not winning deference or wealth.

> “Get money, then,” says some friend, “in order that we too may have it.” **If I can get money and at the same time keep myself self-respecting, and faithful, and high-minded, show me the way and I will get it.** But if you require me to lose the good things that belong to me, in order that you may acquire the things that are not good, you can see for yourselves how unfair and inconsiderate you are.

Epictetus, Oldfather, Handbook24

> Man, you used to be modest, and are no longer so; have you lost nothing? ... **Is not self-respect lost, is not decency lost?** ... Instead of shameless, you will be self-respecting; instead of faithless, faithful; instead of dissolute, self-controlled.

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses4.9

> **do you on that account not care what manner of person you show yourself to be?** ... “keep this man for me unchanged from the character with which nature endowed him — reverent, faithful, high-minded, undismayed, unimpassioned, unperturbed.”

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses2.8

Earning money is allowed here. What conflicts with self-respect is trading this quality away for externals. Oldfather's 'reverent' in 2.8 renders the same Greek word.

Overlaps with faithful and high-minded; the source joins them. Not social status, not wealth refusal. Recovery remains possible (4.9).

## shameless

Handbook 24's named opposite, developed in Discourses 4.9 as self-inflicted loss of self-respect and decency.

> **But if, through your desire to help the State, you lose these qualities, of what good would you become to it, when in the end you turned out to be shameless and unfaithful?**

Epictetus, Oldfather, Handbook24

> Behold, you *have* been dislodged, though by no one else but yourself. Fight against yourself, vindicate yourself for decency, for respect, for freedom. ... **first of all condemn what you are doing; then, when you have passed your condemnation, do not despair of yourself** ... Instead of shameless, you will be self-respecting

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses4.9

Not nudity or custom alone. The source stresses recoverability. Its sexual language is historical source data.

## unfaithful

Handbook 24's named opposite; breaking trust and fidelity (Discourses 2.4, 4.13).

> **if we abandon this fidelity to which we are by nature born, and make designs against our neighbour’s wife, what are we doing? Why, what but ruining and destroying?** ... Are we not overthrowing also neighbourly feeling, friendship, the state? ... And who is going to trust you?

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses2.4

> **if I myself resemble the other person, because I want to get even with him I tell about his affairs, and confound him and am myself confounded.**

Epictetus, Oldfather, Discourses4.13

Adultery and confidences illustrate the vice but do not exhaust it. 'Women common property' is the source's historical analogy, not advice.
