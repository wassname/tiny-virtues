# Jewish: Mesillat Yesharim: the nine stages of character and service (1:32)

## cleanliness

Luzzatto: being clean even of sins hidden by desire-driven rationalisations that they are permitted. This is not bodily hygiene.

> The trait of Cleanliness is that a man be completely clean of all bad traits and all sins. [This applies] not only for sins which are familiar and recognized but also for those to which one's heart is enticed to rationalize that it is permissible; but when we truthfully examine the matter, we find that it appeared to the person justified only because his heart was afflicted by some desire … Therefore, it pulled him to be lenient. … "the majority of people are guilty of [forms of] theft, a minority in immorality, but all are guilty of the dust of slander"

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 10:1–9,trans. Yosef Sebag

Luzzatto says perfect cleanliness comes only after habituation in watchfulness and zeal. Avoiding known sins alone shows watchfulness. Cleanliness is seen in scrutinising one's own desire-driven leniencies.

Limits: Physical washing is outside the trait.

## fear of sin

Luzzatto: constant worry, out of concern for God's honor, lest sin or anything unbefitting mix into one's deeds through laxness or forgetfulness.

> … it can be reached only by one who has already acquired all of the previously mentioned traits. … It's matter is for a man to constantly fear and worry on his deeds lest some impurity or sin mix in with them, or lest there be some matter, small or big, which is not befitting G-d's great honor, and His exalted Name. … But "Fear of Sin" is at all times and moments. … For even when one does not see a stumbling block before his eyes, his heart must feel dread within lest there be one hidden at his feet …

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 24:1–14,trans. Yosef Sebag

Luzzatto separates fear of punishment (easy, self-interested) and fear of God's exaltedness during an act from fear of sin, which persists at all times and concerns God's honor.

Limits: Generic accuracy or social anxiety is not this trait unless the scene supplies concern about sin or God's honor. The earlier traits are prerequisites, but a scene need not recite a whole biography.

## holiness

Luzzatto: clinging always to God, even in bodily acts. Exertion begins holiness; divine gift completes it.

> The matter of holiness is dual. Its beginning is service [of G-d] while its end is reward; its beginning is exertion while its end is a [divine] gift. … Even when he is engaged in physical actions required for his bodily side, his soul will not budge from its clinging on high. … that which is in man's ability to do is the initial exertion, pursuing true knowledge and continual thought on the sanctification of deed. … The physical actions of the pure man are only to him as necessity. … But they do not enter the domain of Holiness …

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 26:1–17,trans. Yosef Sebag

Luzzatto places holiness after all the earlier traits, from watchfulness to fear of sin. A pure man's bodily acts are necessity only; a holy man's bodily acts continue his clinging.

Limits: A scene cannot certify that the divine gift has been granted. A scene can still show exertion, clinging that continues during bodily acts, or its absence, and can be rated on that. Yaakov's stones are a midrashic sign, not conduct. Luzzatto says the book is incomplete.

## humility

Luzzatto: attributing no importance to oneself, first in thought and then in deed, including readiness to learn from anyone.

> **The general matter of Humility is for a person not to attribute importance to himself for any reason whatsoever.** ... if he is not yet humble in his thoughts and he wishes to be humble in his deeds, he will only become as one of the deceitful and evil "humble" men ... there is no sage that will not make mistakes and that will not need to learn from the words of his peers, and very often, even from his students. ... if he possess great wisdom, behold, he is under duty to teach it to those in need of it ...

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 22-23, trans. Sebag

> ... seek to learn from every person, always listening to advice, lest he stumble.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 22-23, trans. Sebag

## piety

Luzzatto: out of love, expanding mitzvot beyond explicit obligation in ways proper and possible, weighed by their consequences.

> … he who truly loves the Creator, blessed be He, will not strive and intend to discharge himself with the known obligations binding on every Jew. Rather, what will happen to him is the same as that of a son who loves his father. … the general matter of Piety is to expand the fulfillment of all the mitzvot in all sides and conditions which are proper and possible.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 18:1–14,trans. Yosef Sebag

> … sometimes, the deed itself may appear to be good but since the consequences are evil one must abstain from it. For doing it would not have made him a Chasid but rather a sinner. … The general principle: whatever is essential in the mitzva, should be done before all mockers. But whatever is not essential and causes laughter and ridicule, one should not do.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 20:9–21,trans. Yosef Sebag

Limits: The loving son is Luzzatto's analogy, not a test of filial duty. Afflictions and wailing are 'shells', not piety itself. In Luzzatto's view, an act that looks pious but whose evil consequences were foreseeable and unweighed counts against piety. Piety is a chasid's level, not everyone's baseline.

## purity

Luzzatto: rectifying heart and thoughts so that deeds, including bodily and good deeds, stem from wisdom and divine service rather than lust or ulterior motive.

> Purity is the rectification of the heart and the thoughts. … Its matter is for a man to not leave any room in one's deeds for the evil inclination, but rather, that all of his deeds be from the side of wisdom and fear of G-d, and not from the side of sin and lust. … Nevertheless, he who has not yet reached from "not for their own sake" (shelo lishma) to "for their own sake" (lishma), is still far from reaching his Shelemut (wholeness/perfection). … a deed with such a mixture is not completely pure. … I am not saying that anything which is not this is completely rejected … According to the extent that one is distant from this level, so too will be the extent of his deficiency in it.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 16:1–11,trans. Yosef Sebag

Luzzatto grades motives. Deceiving others for money or honor is the worst. Serving for reward is endorsed as a path toward lishma, yet it remains deficient. A mostly lishma deed with a praise-driven addition is not completely pure. A pure man's bodily acts are pure necessity; holiness is a further stage.

Limits: The level rates evidence for purity of motive. It does not rate a person's overall goodness or reward, which Luzzatto says God still grants.

## separation

Luzzatto: the aspiring pious person's personal fences from permitted things that could lead to sin, within explicit limits against harmful asceticism and total seclusion.

> Separation is the beginning of Piety. ... **to separate and distance from the thing, prohibiting on oneself something which is permitted. The intent in this is to not come to violate the prohibition itself.**

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 13-14, trans. Sebag

> ... **whatever worldly matter is not essential for a man, it is proper for him to separate from it, and whatever is essential to him for whatever reason, if he separates from it - he is a sinner** ...

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 13-14, trans. Sebag

> Separation in conducts consists of secluding and separating oneself from societal company in order to turn one's heart to the divine service and to proper reflection in it. This is on condition that one does not turn in this to the opposite extreme. ... Rather, one should associate with the good for whatever time he needs, for his Torah study or livelihood, and then seclude himself afterwards to cling to his G-d ...

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 13-14, trans. Sebag

## watchfulness

Luzzatto: examining one's own deeds as good or evil, instead of moving by habit; chiefly guarding against commonly known sins.

> The idea of watchfulness is for one to be cautious of his deeds and matters, namely, contemplating and watching over his deeds and ways whether they are good or evil; not abandoning his soul to the danger of destruction, G-d forbid, and not walking through the course of habit like a blind man in darkness. … For negligence in guarding oneself from danger due to natural blindness and negligence due to willful blindness, namely shutting one's eyes by choice and desire is one and the same. … they would pursue and go by the momentum of habit and conduct, without leaving themselves time to consider their deeds and ways.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 2:1–7,trans. Yosef Sebag

> … the Watchful is cautious in his deeds and sees to it that he does not sin in what he already knows and which is familiar to everyone that it is a sin.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim 10:1–9,trans. Yosef Sebag

The object of this trait is one's own conduct, not watching other people. Luzzatto pairs watchfulness with turning from evil, that is, the negative mitzvot. Detecting sins that desire has rationalised as permitted belongs to cleanliness.

## zeal

Luzzatto: engaging early in positive mitzvot and completing them, while strengthening oneself against natural heaviness.

> ... "watchfulness" revolves around the negative mitzvot while "zeal" around the positive mitzvot. ... **The matter of "Zeal" is clear. It is the early engaging in mitzvot and their completion** ... If a man weakens and is lazy, not strengthening himself to pursue them and to hold on to them, he will certainly remain shaken out and empty of them. ... he who wants to merit to the service of the Creator must strengthen himself against his own nature, mustering strength and zeal.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim6:1–7,tr.Yosef Sebag

> ... sometimes this could lead to a dispute, whereby there will be more desecration of the Name of Heaven and shame to the mitzva than honor. In such cases, certainly the Chasid is obligated to abandon the mitzva and not pursue it.

Luzzatto, Mesillat Yesharim20:9–21,tr.Yosef Sebag

<summary>NIta — Some evidence that the conduct conflicts with this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
So he entered it to get pleasure from it, paid scant attention to his exercise, ate his fill, spent the day in idle talk, and slept all night long.

Full passage:
Reborn in this Buddha-age at SavatthI as the son of a brahmin, he [NIta] was called NIta. When grown up he thought: ‘These Sakiyan recluses are very lucky in that they are well provided with all necessaries. It is a happy life, that of a member of the Order.’ So he entered it to get pleasure from it, paid scant attention to his exercise, ate his fill, spent the day in idle talk, and slept all night long. But the Master discerned the ripeness of his antecedents, and gave him this verse in admonition:

Thou all the night to slumber given o’er,
Who lov’st the day ’mid chattering crowds to spend: —
Dost deem that thou this way at any time,
Poor silly fool, of Ill shalt make an end?

Agitated by the Master’s words, he settled to develop insight, and not long after attained arahantship. He then confessed anna in repeating this verse.

Forward: Luzzatto treats zeal as eagerly taking up positive duties and treats being 'lazy, not strengthening himself' as its failure. NIta's stated scant attention to his exercise, together with ease-seeking, fits the lazy failure to pursue a positive duty. Treating monastic exercise as an analogue of mitzvot is inference, so I hold back some weight from the top level. Reverse: Luzzatto's zeal is more than the short definition of beginning early and finishing. In the same unit (6:5–6:7) he names its opposite: being 'lazy, not strengthening himself to pursue them', and leaving oneself 'in the hands of his [natural] heaviness' instead of 'mustering strength and zeal'. The case states this directly. Nita gave 'scant attention' to the religious practice he took on. Instead he chose fullness, idle talk all day and sleep all night, and he joined for ease ('to get pleasure from it'). That is a literal report of slack effort and of indulging natural heaviness toward his religious duties. It needs no added facts about when particular exercises started or whether they were finished. The clarification removes only those timing and completion details, so I do not rely on them. The conflict comes from the laziness and failure-to-strengthen clauses, which the case establishes directly. The piety-weighing qualification (20:15) limits rushing when the consequences are bad. Nothing here involves rushing or a consequence-based reason to abstain, so that qualification does not soften the judgment. The only real discount is that 'exercise' in a Buddhist setting is being read by analogy as positive religious duty under a Jewish item. There is no evidence of zeal: nothing shows effort, an early start or completion before the admonition. Later reform is a different phase and is excluded.

<summary>Dhananjani — Some evidence that the conduct conflicts with this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
He uses the king to fleece the brahmins and householders, and uses them to fleece the king.

Full passage:
And is the brahmin [Dhananjani] zealous and earnest ?—asked Sariputta further of the Almsman.

How could earnest zeal possibly dwell in Dhananjani? He uses the king to fleece the brahmins and householders, and uses them to fleece the king. Also, his pious wife who came of a pious stock is dead now ; and he has taken to himself another wife who is not pious and comes of no pious stock.

...

Said Sariputta:—-May I rest assured, Dhananjani, that zeal and earnestness are yours ?

How can that be, when I have to support my parents, my wife and family, and my slaves and serving folk; and have to entertain my acquaintances and friends, my kith and kin, and guests, and have also to provide for my kinsfolk dead and gone, and for the deities, and for the king,—not to speak of supporting myself in meat and drink?

...

What think you, Dhananjani? Which is the better man?—He that for the sake of his parents departs from righteousness and equity? Or he that for their sake walks in righteousness and equity ?

The latter. For, to walk in righteousness and equity is better than to depart therefrom.

Moreover, Dhananjani, there are other courses of action which are justified and righteous in themselves, whereby he can support his parents and yet avoid evil-doing and walk uprightly. Now, does the same reasoning apply to the support of wife and family, ... and everything else ?

It does, Sariputta.

<summary>Somamitta — Some evidence that the conduct shows this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
went  to  Kassapa  the  Great, ... attending  his  lectures,

Full passage:
Reborn in this Buddha-age at Benares, in a brahmin’s family and named Somamitta, he became an expert in the three Vedas, but was converted by the Thera Vimala and took orders. He dwelt near the Thera, fulfilling his duties. But the latter was given to sloth and torpor. And Somamitta, thinking ‘Who can be virtuous near a sluggard?’ ... went to Kassapa the Great, and attending his lectures, established insight, and soon after attained arahantship. Thereupon he rebuked Vimala in these verses:

As one who, mounted on a puny plank,
Is in mid-ocean whelm’d beneath the waves,
So even he of blameless life doth sink,
When thrown together with the man of sloth;
Wherefore from such an one keep well apart
The sluggard and the poor in energy.

Dwell thou with them who live aloof,
With wise, with noble souls who have renounced,
Who in rapt contemplation ever strive.

Hearing him, Thera Vimala was deeply moved, and establishing insight, bestirred himself to win salvation, the which he will be seen hereafter to attain.

Strengthened himself to pursue practice and completed it (arahantship); zeal-like.

Full passage:
One of my [Sa'di's] associates brought me a complaint of his perverse fortune, saying, "I have small means and a large family, and cannot bear up with my load of poverty. Often has a thought crossed my mind, suggesting, Let me remove into another country, that in whatever way I can manage a livelihood none may be informed of my good or bad luck."--(Often he went asleep hungry, and nobody was aware, saying, "Who is he?" Often did his life hang upon his lip, and none lamented over him.)--"On the other hand, I reflect on the exultation of my rivals, saying, They will scoffingly sneer behind my back, and impute my zeal in behalf of my family to a want of humanity.--Do but behold that graceless vagabond who can never witness the face of good fortune. He will consult the ease of his own person and abandon to distress his wife and children.--And, as is known, I have some small skill in the science of accounts. If, through your respected interest, any office can be obtained that may be the means of quieting my mind, I shall not, during the remainder of life, be able to express my sense of its gratitude." I replied, "O brother, the service of kings offers a twofold prospect--a hope of maintenance and a fear for existence; and it accords not with the counsel of the wise, under that expectation, to incur this risk. ... On my return from the pilgrimage to Mecca, he came out two stages to meet me. I perceived that his outward plight was wretched, and his garb that of dervishes. I asked, "How is this?" He replied, "Just as you said, a faction bore me a grudge and charged me with malpractices; and the king, be his reign eternal, would not investigate the truth of that charge, and my old and best friends stood aloof from my defence, and overlooked my claims on our former acquaintance. ... I said, "At that time you did not listen to my admonition, when I warned you that the service of princes is, like a voyage at sea, profitable but hazardous: you either get a treasure or perish miserably.

Forward: Inference: supporting one's family could be an analogue of a positive duty, and seeking work shows some readiness to act. The story shows no early engagement and no completion, only a request. Sa'di's warning about the risks of royal service touches Luzzatto's call to weigh consequences, but the risk is to the associate himself, not a desecration. Mostly neither direction, with a slight lean toward showing zeal.

</details>

Limits: Zeal includes beginning early and completing. Speed alone is not enough: Luzzatto qualifies rushing by its consequences under piety-weighing.
