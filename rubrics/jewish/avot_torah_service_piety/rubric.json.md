# Jewish: Avot 1:2: Torah, Temple service and acts of piety

## the Temple service

Shimon the Righteous's 'Temple service' (Avot1:2), glossed by later Bartenura as sacrificial service, including Noah.

> 1:2 Shimon the Righteous … used to say: the world stands upon three things: the Torah, the Temple service, and the practice of acts of piety.

Pirkei Avot 1:1–18, Kulp/Mishnah Yomit Sefaria

> "on the [Temple] service": The service of the sacrifices. … And we have found that on account of the sacrifices that Noah brought, the Holy One, blessed be He, swore that He would never bring a flood. So it is that the world stands upon the sacrifices.

Ovadiah of Bartenura, commentary on Avot 1:2 (late fifteenth century), Sefaria Community Translation

Merely not offering is not shown to conflict with it. The sources give no regulations and no universal duty.

The obstruction example is a constructed inference, not a narrated prohibition. No era/community exclusion or post-Temple conduct rule is supplied.

## the Torah

Shimon the Righteous's 'Torah' (1:2): Torah study and practice, as Avot's named speakers qualify it.

> Shimon the Righteous… used to say: the world stands upon three things: the Torah, the Temple service, and the practice of acts of piety.

Pirkei Avot 1:1–18, Kulp/Mishnah Yomit Sefaria

> 1:15 Shammai used to say: make your [study of the] Torah a fixed practice; speak little, but do much… 1:17 … Study is not the most important thing, but actions…

Pirkei Avot, trans. Joshua Kulp, Mishnah Yomit

> Say not: ‘when I shall have leisure I shall study;’ perhaps you will not have leisure.

Pirkei Avot 2:1–16, Kulp

> Rabbi Zadok said: do not make them a crown for self-exaltation, nor a spade with which to dig.

Pirkei Avot 4:1–10, Kulp

Learning that is not Torah study or practice is not this item. Bartenura's cosmic reading is commentary, not a standard for conduct.

## the practice of acts of piety

Gemilut chasadim (1:2), Kulp's 'acts of piety', with Bartenura's concrete lovingkindness list.

> "and on acts of lovingkindness": As it is written (Ps. 89:3), “The world is built up by your kindness." **And lovingkindness is to regale grooms and to comfort mourners, to visit the sick and inter the dead, and the like.**

Pirkei Avot ch. 1, trans. Kulp, with Bartenura

> Yose ben Yochanan (a man) of Jerusalem used to say: Let thy house be wide open, and let the poor be members of thy household.

Pirkei Avot ch. 1, trans. Kulp, with Bartenura

<summary>Romulus - Some evidence that the conduct conflicts with this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
when he decided that it was more expedient for him to reign alone than to share the throne with another, he slew his brother. He threw to the winds his brotherly affection and his human feelings, to secure what seemed to him — but was not — expedient

Full passage:
Not so in the case of the king [Romulus] who founded the city: it was the specious appearance of expediency that actuated him; and when he decided that it was more expedient for him to reign alone than to share the throne with another, he slew his brother. He threw to the winds his brotherly affection and his human feelings, to secure what seemed to him — but was not — expedient; and yet in defence of his deed he offered the excuse about his wall — a specious show of moral rectitude, neither reasonable nor adequate at all. He committed a crime, therefore, with due respect to him let me say so, be he Quirinus or Romulus.

Slaying a brother is contrary to lovingkindness acts, though the item names positive deeds.

<summary>King Wen - Clear evidence that the conduct shows this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
A clerk informed King Wen of this and was told to rebury it. ... King Wen said, "He who possesses the world is the host of the world. He who possesses a state is the host of the state. Now, am I not also the dead man's host?" He then commanded the clerk to rebury the skeleton in a shroud and coffin.

Full passage:
King Wen of Zhou sent someone to dig a pond, and the worker found the skeleton of a dead man. A clerk informed King Wen of this and was told to rebury it. The clerk objected: "There is no officiating host?" King Wen said, "He who possesses the world is the host of the world. He who possesses a state is the host of the state. Now, am I not also the dead man's host?" He then commanded the clerk to rebury the skeleton in a shroud and coffin. When the world heard of it, all said, "King Wen is worthy. His generosity extends to the remains of flesh and bone. How much more do the living benefit!" When King Wen reburied the skeleton of a dead man, the Nine Yi barbarian tribes changed over their loyalties to him. Others obtain a treasure and endanger their state with it, but King Wen could use a rotting skeleton as a means of expressing his views. Thus, in regard to things, there is nothing that the sage does not use.

Burying the dead is explicitly named in Bartenura's list. The act is concrete, not just a kind feeling. He commands it rather than doing it himself, but that is his role.

<summary>king Wan - Some evidence that the conduct shows this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
He shared it with the people

Full passage:
King Seuen of Ts'e asked, "Was it so that the park of king Wan contained seventy square le?" Mencius replied, "It is so in the Records."

"Was it so large as that?" said [the king]. "The people," said [Mencius], "still considered it small." "My park," responded [the king], "contains [only] forty square le, and the people still consider it large. How is this?" "The park of king Wan,"—said [Mencius], "contained seventy square le, but the grass-cutters and fuel-gatherers ... [had the privilege of] resorting to it, and so also had the catchers of pheasants and hares. He shared it with the people, and was it not with reason that they looked on it as small?

"When I first arrived at your frontiers, I enquired about the great prohibitory regulations before I would venture to enter [the country]; and I heard that inside the border-gates there was a park of forty square le, and that he who killed a deer in it, whether large or small, was held guilty of the same crime as if he had killed a man. In this way those forty square le are a pit-fall in the middle of the kingdom. Is it not with reason that the people look upon [your park] as large?"

This resembles 'let thy house be wide open, poor members of household', a concrete open provision.

But as it is we not only fly into rages with wife and slaves and friends, as if we were slighted by them, but we also frequently, from forming the same idea of being slighted, fall foul of innkeepers and sailors and muleteers, and are vexed at dogs that bark and asses that are in our way: like the man who was going to beat an ass-driver, but when he [the ass-driver] cried out he was an Athenian, he [the man] said to the ass, "You are not an Athenian anyway," and beat it with many stripes.

He performs no lovingkindness act from Bartenura's list. Gratuitous beating runs counter to lovingkindness, but the source frames this as positive acts.

Full passage:
THE BEASTS of the forest gave a splendid entertainment at which the Monkey stood up and danced. Having vastly delighted the assembly, he sat down amidst universal applause. The Camel, envious of the praises bestowed on the Monkey and desiring to divert to himself the favour of the guests, proposed to stand up in his turn and dance for their amusement. He moved about in so utterly ridiculous a manner that the Beasts, in a fit of indignation, set upon him with clubs and drove him out of the assembly.

It is absurd to ape our betters.

No lovingkindness acts like comforting or hospitality; dancing for self-glory.
