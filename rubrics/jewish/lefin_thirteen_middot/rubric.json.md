# Jewish: The thirteen illustrative training traits of Cheshbon HaNefesh

## Calmness

Lefin's calmness: calm, considered speech and dealings that avoid arousing hatred; strictness without overstepping bounds.

> The words of the wise are stated gently. In being good, do not be called evil.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh XI, trans. Landesman/Silverstein

> Therefore, one must be very careful in his negotiations with others. **He should carefully and calmly consider matters before he speaks or acts so that he does not arouse animosity, hatred or strife, God forbid.** All of one's dealings and communications with others should be carried out in a calm atmosphere. Shouting and anger are only fitting for fools and the wicked. Even at those times when one must act strictly with others, one should still make every attempt not to overstep the bounds.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh XI, trans. Landesman/Silverstein

Lefin's 'narrow parameters' also condemn indifference to others' opinions; calmness is not detachment.

## Cleanliness

Lefin's cleanliness: no stain or ugliness on body, clothes, possessions or home; rooted in Torah exhortation and human dignity.

> Let no stain or ugliness be found in your possessions or in your home, and surely not on your body or clothes.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh V, §§84–86, pp 141–143; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> The entire Torah — written and that which is orally transmitted — is replete with exhortations to cleanliness and spiritual purity.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh V, §§84–86, pp 141–143; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> If this is the way that you are affected by the shortcomings of others, then you had better remember to take care of yourself. Don’t say, “I am preoccupied with Heavenly matters. How can I waste my time with bodily matters just for the sake of appearances?” Our Sages said (MENACHOS 37b): Human dignity is great, for it overrides a negative precept of the Torah.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh V, §§84–86, pp 141–143; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

Lefin's stated conclusion from the stranger's appearance is to take care of yourself. Nested Talmudic sayings remain the Sages' attribution.

## Decisiveness

Lefin's decisiveness: brief probabilistic deliberation, then prompt, vigorous execution; both haste and endless hesitation stray.

> All of your acts should be preceded by deliberation; when you have reached a decision, act without hesitating.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IV, §§81–83, pp 135–139; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> Generally, one must be content to rely on logic and make his determination based on probability. Moreover, one must reach his logical decision quickly, for most situations require alacrity so that the opportune moment does not pass. One must be stubborn so as to overcome all the impediments that stand in the way of making a decision.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IV, §§81–83, pp 135–139; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> There are people whose process of deliberation is so short that it seems as if they conduct all their affairs solely according to the advice of their animal spirits. … When faced with a situation with which they are unfamiliar, they think and reflect and ponder, then they consult and think again interminably.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IV, §§81–83, pp 135–139; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> A person is considering whether he should strike up a friendship with someone else so as to study Torah … He continues to deliberate and hesitate because of people who obstruct him or mock him — and, in the meantime, the other person finds someone else.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IV, §§81–83, pp 135–139; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

Lefin admits outcomes can rarely be known precisely, so a later bad result does not by itself show conflict. The translation's 'obstinancy' is retained as printed.

## Diligence

Lefin's diligence: always finding worthwhile activity for oneself or a friend, without wasting time on idleness or excess animal pleasure.

> **Always find something to do — for yourself or for a friend, and don’t allow a moment of your life to be wasted.**

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IX, §§93–95, pp161–163; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim1995

> Hence, he has no choice but to cede some of his time so as to dispose his animal spirit to seek to fulfill the Divine service. Nevertheless, the bulk of his time must be directed towards regaling in the delights of the supernal soul, progressively perfecting himself in this area daily.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IX, §§93–95, pp161–163; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim1995

> Our reference in this section is not only to complete ... idleness ... but even to the short amount of time which man spends on the pleasures of the yetzer bara beyond that called for by his Divine obligations. This too can be seen as “idleness”

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh IX, §§93–95, pp161–163; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim1995

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
So he entered it to get pleasure from it, paid scant attention to his exercise, ate his fill, spent the day in idle talk, and slept all night long.

Full passage:
Reborn in this Buddha-age at SavatthI as the son of a brahmin, he [NIta] was called NIta. When grown up he thought: ‘These Sakiyan recluses are very lucky in that they are well provided with all necessaries. But the Master discerned the ripeness of his antecedents, and gave him this verse in admonition:

Thou all the night to slumber given o’er,
Who lov’st the day ’mid chattering crowds to spend: —
Dost deem that thou this way at any time,
Poor silly fool, of Ill shalt make an end?

Forward: Lefin counts even short indulgence beyond what obligations require as idleness. The story states as fact that NIta spends whole days in idle talk, eats his fill and sleeps all night, and that he entered the Order 'to get pleasure from it'. This is a direct case of wasted time and animal pleasure. Lefin's allowance for needed rest does not cover a pattern explicitly linked to seeking pleasure and neglecting his exercise. Reverse: Lefin counts even a short time spent on animal pleasures beyond one's obligations as idleness. NIta's whole day ('spent the day in idle talk') and night go to talk, food and sleep, while his actual practice gets 'scant attention'. That is the wasted time the trait opposes. The limit protecting necessary rest does not apply, because the rest is not in service of returning to a task. The task is the thing being neglected. Clear conflict.

How could earnest zeal possibly dwell in Dhananjani? He uses the king to fleece the brahmins and householders, and uses them to fleece the king. Also, his pious wife who came of a pious stock is dead now ; and he has taken to himself another wife who is not pious and comes of no pious stock.

...

How can that be, when I have to support my parents, my wife and family, and my slaves and serving folk; and have to entertain my acquaintances and friends, my kith and kin, and guests, and have also to provide for my kinsfolk dead and gone, and for the deities, and for the king,—not to speak of supporting myself in meat and drink?

What think you, Dhananjani? Which is the better man?—He that for the sake of his parents departs from righteousness and equity? Or he that for their sake walks in righteousness and equity ?

## Equanimity

Lefin's equanimity: keeping the mind settled amid inconsequential good or bad events, news, pain, pleasure and changes in routine.

> Rise above events that are inconsequential — both bad and good — for they are not worth disturbing your equanimity.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh I, §§67–71, pp 109–115; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> Similarly, there are people who become unsettled by their fears at night. … The remedy is to condition himself — to approach whatever has disturbed him, to touch and feel it before his imagination has the time to enshroud it in illusion.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh I, §§67–71, pp 109–115; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> Other people lose their mental balance when they are overburdened; e.g., when they have to perform some kind of necessary duty, they become curt and short-tempered with their families. … Their cure? They have to condition themselves to act patiently and calmly as well as to reinforce the trait of order (see Section III) and other similar traits.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh I, §§67–71, pp 109–115; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

Lefin's nerve and animal-spirit physiology is a historical source claim.

## Frugality

Lefin's frugality, 'loving honestly earned money': no needless spending, especially prestige squandering; frugal with self, generous to others.

> (loving honestly earned money) Be careful with your money. Do not spend even a penny needlessly.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VIII, §§91–92, pp 153–159; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> Every day they devise new ways to waste their money — on clothing and jewelry or giving larger sums to charity than others do — all to embarrass those who are without means and to gain prestige for themselves. Although they have already exhausted the credit available to them, they continue to spend and squander

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VIII, §§91–92, pp 153–159; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> And who is honorable? One who honors his fellow man, who is frugal with himself and generous to others.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VIII, §§91–92, pp 153–159; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> better is be who gives a small amount of charity from funds that are his, than one who steals and robs and cheats and gives more charity than others.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VIII, §§91–92, pp 153–159; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

Generous charity is not itself needless spending; Lefin's objection concerns prestige and money that is not one's own. The miser boundary is a source gap.

## Humility

Lefin's humility: learning from everyone, correcting one's own failings, and not dwelling on one's own virtues or others' faults.

> Always seek to learn wisdom from every man, to recognize your failings and correct them. In doing so you will learn to stop thinking about your virtues and you will take your mind off your friend's faults.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VI, §§87–89, pp 145–147; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> This is the prime enterprise of the yetzer ha-ra: to have the desire for real honor degenerate into the pursuit of honor. The point is reached where one resorts to various subterfuges and deceptions so that others might see virtues in him which he lacks! Or, he may seek out the company of men of lesser stature so that he can relish his own superiority.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VI, §§87–89, pp 145–147; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> This malady requires reverse conditioning; i.e., one must accustom himself to remain silent even when in the company of men of lesser stature … He must train himself to elicit new ideas from their speech and actions which will be beneficial in correcting his traits. … He must also be aghast when he sees his friend taking pride in a minor, degrading trait and remember that he too suffers from that same trait

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh VI, §§87–89, pp 145–147; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

Lefin says honor-desire was implanted for a purpose, so enjoying deserved admiration is not automatically conflict; the malady is its degeneration.

## Order

Lefin's order: set place and time for actions, possessions and thoughts; attention on the present task.

> All your actions and possessions should be orderly — each and every one in a set place and at a set time. Let your thoughts always be free to deal with that which lies ahead of you.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh III, §§78–80, pp 129–133; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> This is the trait of order; allocating a set time for each and every thought and analysis, freeing time and space for each and every affair in the world of action, and demarcating the bounds of each with set boundaries so that one not intrude upon the other.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh III, §§78–80, pp 129–133; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

> There are some whose household utensils and appurtenances are piled atop each other or scattered about … so that some can not be found when they are needed, or precious time is wasted searching for them … There are people who are engrossed in halachic analysis or in making their business accountings while reciting the amidab. … [One] must condition himself to focus all of his attention on what he is doing at that moment.

Lefin, Cheshbon ha-Nefesh III, §§78–80, pp 129–133; Landesman/Silverstein English edition, Feldheim 1995

The dream-process psychology is a historical claim. Mere tidiness for display is not shown here as the trait; Lefin's focus is boundaries and freed attention.
