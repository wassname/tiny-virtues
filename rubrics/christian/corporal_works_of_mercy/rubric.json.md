# Christian: Seven corporal works of mercy (Penny Catechism)

## to bury the dead

Gibson: laying the dead in the earth with reverence, as the body awaits resurrection.

> Therefore, we should treat them throughout life with due respect, and **after death they should be laid with reverence in the earth to await the final resurrection.** Hence it is that the religious burial of the dead has always been an object of the tender care of the Church.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p243

> ... the history of Tobias, who is especially praised in the Holy Scripture, because at great personal sacrifice and peril " he was careful to bury the dead and those that were slain."

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p243

There is no requirement of an elaborate funeral. Gibson's resurrection and Tobias claims are attributed teaching.

## to clothe the naked

Gibson: supplying clothing needed against disease and exposure, according to one's means.

> ... **without proper clothing and shelter he will easily contract disease, and perish from exposure to the wet and cold.** Hence it is, that those who exercise themselves in works of charity, usually devote themselves in the first place to the supply of these most pressing wants.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p241

> When you feed the Hungry, you feed Jesus Christ; when you clothe the naked, you clothe Jesus Christ

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p244

> warns us against the danger of spoiling the merit of our good actions by an unworthy motive, for example, that of vain glory or a desire to be esteemed by men.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p244

Fashion or gift-giving among the comfortable is not this work. Gibson's merit claims are attributed. His vainglory warning applies to the corporal works, so it overlaps with this card.

## to feed the hungry

Gibson: relieving actual hunger according to one's means, not mere pity.

> Food, and clothing, and shelter, my dear children, are the first and principal corporal wants of man. Without suitable and sufficient food his body will soon waste away ... **For they are not content with pitying the necessities of the poor, but apply themselves according to their means to relieve them**; otherwise they would incur the reproach of St. James, " My brethren, if a brother or sister be naked and want daily food, and one of you say to him, Go in peace, be you warmed and filled, yet give them not those things which are necessary for the body, what shall it profit?"

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p241

> Whence we learn, that if we wish our alms to be meritorious in the sight of God, we must carefully avoid seeking the applause of men, being moved only by the pure motive of pleasing Almighty God.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p244

> warns us against the danger of spoiling the merit of our good actions by an unworthy motive, for example, that of vain glory or a desire to be esteemed by men.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p244

Means matter: inability is not refusal. Gibson's merit claims are attributed. His motive warning is addressed to the corporal works themselves, so it overlaps with this card.

## to give drink to the thirsty

Gibson pairs this with feeding the hungry: practical relief of bodily need according to one's means.

> To feed the hungry and to give drink to the thirsty are the first two Corporal Works of Mercy here mentioned. ... **For they are not content with pitying the necessities of the poor, but apply themselves according to their means to relieve them**

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p241

> And if to him who gives even a cup of cold water in his name, our Lord has promised an abundant reward

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Spiritual Works of Mercy,pp249–250

Gibson gives no separate explanation of drink. Its teaching is shared with feeding.

## to harbour the harbourless

Gibson: giving shelter and a home to those who have none.

> ... **and the fourth to harbour the harbourless, that is, to give shelter and a home to those who have none.** ... without proper clothing and shelter he will easily contract disease, and perish from exposure to the wet and cold.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p241

> " ... and setting him upon his own beast brought him to an inn, and took care of him. And the next day he took out two pence, and gave to the host, and said, Take care of him"

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,pp239–240

Gibson gives only the gloss plus shared teaching. Inability to host is not adverse.

## to visit the imprisoned

Gibson: relieving prisoners' wants, aiding the falsely accused, and helping released prisoners into honest work.

> To visit and relieve the wants of these unhappy creatures was always considered in the Church as a great act of charity ... In this country few except the clergy themselves are permitted to enter the prisons; moreover, the prisoners are provided with all that they require during the time of their confinement. **It may, however, be sometime in our power to assist those who are falsely accused to defend themselves at their trial, and it is always a great charity to help the poor prisoners on coming out of gaol to obtain honest employment.**

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,pp242–243

This is not limited to literal visits. Gibson's historical claims are attributed; no judgment on guilt is implied.

## to visit the sick

Gibson: nursing the sick and providing comforts and remedies to restore health or to lighten pain and tediousness. Spiritual consolation is urged as an addition.

> **Speaking of this as a corporal work, it refers to the nursing of the sick, and the providing them with those comforts and remedies which will tend to restore the health of their body, or at least to lighten the pains and tediousness of their sickness.** But we must not confine ourselves to the relief of their bodily wants when we visit the sick, we must make it also a Spiritual Work of Mercy by consoling and encouraging them in their sufferings

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,pp241–242

Remedies are not the only form of this work: comforts that lighten tediousness, and consolation, count. A brief visit is not scored as the work unless the scene shows a need met or a comfort given. The priest and Levite case is a source contrast, not a statement of their motives.
