# Christian: Four sins crying to heaven for vengeance (Penny Catechism)

## defrauding labourers of their wages

Gibson: keeping back by fraud the hire of labourers, such as reapers.

> **And finally, as to defrauding labourers of their wages, Almighty God announced to the Jews that the hire of the labourers who had reaped their fields, and which by fraud had been kept back, cried out to him, and that the cry had entered into his ears.** ... My dear children, let us detest these hateful crimes

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Four Sins Crying to Heaven,pp274–275

Limits: Fraud is key: honest disputes or inability to pay are not established fraud. Claims of divine vengeance are attributed.

## oppression of the poor

Gibson: cruel oppression of the poor, especially the widow and orphan.

> **In like manner did God declare to the Jews, by the mouth of his prophets, that their cruel oppression of the poor, especially of the widow and orphan, would surely bring down upon them the Divine chastisements**; " The Lord," said he, " will not accept any person against a poor man, and he will hear the prayer of him that is wronged." And again, " The widow's tears run down the cheek — from the cheek they go up even to heaven. "

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Four Sins Crying to Heaven,pp274–275

Limits: Gibson gives the victims and the prophetic warnings but no detailed means. His claims of divine chastisement are attributed.

## sodomy

Gibson's second sin crying to heaven, with the boundary of the term taken from Aquinas's Summa, the standard scholastic definition, and a parallel catechism (Spirago). The verdict is these texts', recorded as taught.

> The next is the sin of Sodomy, an unnatural sin of impurity, which brought down from heaven upon the inhabitants of the wicked city of Sodom a rain of fire and brimstone, that blotted ... them out from the face of the earth.

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III, pp. 274-275

> The sin of Sodom takes its name from the inhabitants of Sodom, who were guilty of unnatural sins, by reason of which they were destroyed by God, Who rained down upon them brimstone and fire (Gen. xix. 24).

Spirago, The Catechism Explained (1899), p. 461

> secondly, because, in addition, it is contrary to the natural order of the venereal act as becoming to the human race: and this is called "the unnatural vice." This may happen in several ways. First, by procuring pollution, without any copulation, for the sake of venereal pleasure ... Secondly, by copulation with a thing of undue species, and this is called "bestiality." Thirdly, by copulation with an undue sex, male with male, or female with female, as the Apostle states (Romans 1:27): and this is called the "vice of sodomy." Fourthly, by not observing the natural manner of copulation

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.154 a.11

> six species are unfittingly assigned to lust, namely, "simple fornication, adultery, incest, seduction, rape, and the unnatural vice."

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.154 a.1, objection 1

> There we enumerated the species of lust that are not contrary to human nature: wherefore the unnatural vice was omitted.

Aquinas, Summa Theologiae II-II q.154 a.11, reply 1

> every sin of impurity even of thought only, is, if fully consented to, grievous in the sight of God

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy II, p. 209

Gibson calls it 'an unnatural sin of impurity' and does not say more; Spirago speaks of the Sodomites' 'unnatural sins'. Aquinas narrows the 'vice of sodomy' to sexual union with one of the same sex, one of four unnatural vices. Other unchaste acts, such as adultery, are impurity but not this sin. Under Gibson's teaching, desire fully consented to is grievous, so refusing it resists the sin.

Limits: Gibson's own term may be wider than Aquinas's narrow 'vice of sodomy'; the two are kept apart, not merged. This records the catechism's teaching, not a modern legal, medical or moral standard. Genesis 19's narrative details are not in Gibson's text and are not used.

## wilful murder

Gibson's first crying sin: wilful murder, exemplified by Cain. Wilfulness distinguishes it from death in general.

> These, my dear children, are four terrible crimes, which, as the Holy Scripture declares, cry to heaven for vengeance, and of which we should therefore entertain a special horror. **The first named is Wilful Murder, on account of which Almighty God declared to Cain that the voice of his brother's blood cried to him from the earth, and that he should therefore be accursed among men.**

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Four Sins Crying to Heaven,pp274–275

Gibson does not define the edges of wilfulness, such as accident or defence. Those boundaries are a source gap.

Limits: Claims about vengeance are attributed. These examples are not modern legal standards.
