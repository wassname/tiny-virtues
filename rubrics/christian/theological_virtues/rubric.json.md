# Christian: Three theological virtues (Penny Catechism)

## charity

Gibson: love of God above all, and of neighbour for God's sake, shown in deeds. It is not merely donating.

> The object of the virtue of Charity, my dear children, is God, either as considered in Himself or as seen in his creatures. **God is also the motive of Charity.** ... we love our neighbour because he is the work of God's hands, and because God himself loves him, and has commanded the same to us.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp227–228

> " And if I should distribute all my goods to feed the poor, and if I should deliver my body to be burnt, and have not charity, it profiteth me nothing."

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp227–228

> These Works of Mercy are, indeed, the test of true charity, for as he, who truly loves God, keepeth his commandments and doth his Holy Will, so does he, who truly loves his neighbour for the sake of God, do all in his power to relieve him in his corporal and spiritual wants.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,pp239–240

Gibson makes charity depend on faith and hope, and says it can be lost by mortal sin.

> warns us against the danger of spoiling the merit of our good actions by an unworthy motive, for example, that of vain glory or a desire to be esteemed by men. " When thou dost an alms-deed," he says, " sound not a trumpet before thee as the hypocrites do in the synagogues and in the streets, that they may be honoured by men.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Corporal Works of Mercy,p244

Not every helpful act shows theological charity. An unknown motive stays uncertain, not adverse. A stated vainglorious motive is scene evidence of an 'unworthy motive', but Gibson does not call it the loss of charity.

## faith

Gibson: divine Faith, meaning firm belief in God's revealed teaching as the Church proposes it. It is not generic trust.

> If you believe it to be true because man tells it you, it is a mere human faith, but if you believe it on the word of God, it is Divine Faith, which is the first of the three Theological virtues, and the foundation of the other two. **This Divine Faith excludes all doubt or hesitation; it commands the entire submission of the understanding to the Divine teaching**, for though man might deceive us, God cannot

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp219–221

> Hence we are bound to receive at once whatever the Church proposes to our belief, as the infallible word of God himself.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp219–221

> ... he will demand an account at the day of Judgment, not only if you have abused and wasted it, but also if you have buried it in the ground by neglecting to multiply it by frequent acts and to make it the rule of your daily lives.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp219–221

Gibson's Judas shows that belief can coexist with despair: 'Faith may exist without Hope'. Gibson files Judas's failure under despair. He does not say whether Judas made faith the rule of his life.

> Hence there can be no Hope without Faith, though Faith may exist without Hope. Thus a man who believes in the truths of religion, may fall into despair, as Judas did, who no doubt believed that Jesus was the Son of God

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp222–224

Gibson's ecclesial claims are attributed, not endorsed. Ordinary trust is not divine Faith, and the absence of religious words is not unbelief. Faith has a lived side ('rule of your daily lives'). Do not score misconduct against faith unless the source or scene links it to faith rather than to hope or charity. Sins opposed to faith are deferred to an instruction that is not supplied.

## hope

Gibson: confident expectation of salvation and its means from God, on stated conditions. It is not worldly optimism.

> Thus a man who believes in the truths of religion, may fall into despair, as Judas did, who no doubt believed that Jesus was the Son of God, come into the world to save mankind, and who yet lost all hope at the thought of his own grievous sin in betraying his Divine Master, and went and hung himself in despair.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp222–224

> The condition for obtaining eternal life is that we keep his commandments; the condition for obtaining God's grace, which will enable us to keep his commandments, is that we seek it by Prayer and the Sacraments.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp222–224

> **Eternal life, therefore, and the means to obtain it, are the proper object of our Hope** ... Hence we may hope for God's pardon if we truly repent; ... On the other hand, we have no reason to hope for the goods of this world, for power, riches, honours, &c, except as far as God sees them useful or necessary to our souls.

Rev.Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy,vol.III(1877), Theological Virtues,pp222–224

Gibson defines presumption as 'expecting some grace without our taking the proper means to obtain it'. He says presumption is opposed to Hope.

> The first two of these sins, Presumption and Despair, are opposed to the virtue of Hope. Presumption is an excess of Hope, in other words, it is hoping to receive from God what we have no right to expect, or expecting some grace without our taking the proper means to obtain it.

Gibson,Catechism Made EasyIII(1877),pp271–272;Presumption/Despair

Gibson's salvation teaching is attributed. Worldly optimism is outside scope. Presumption is defined in Gibson’s discussion of presumption (pp. 271–272).
