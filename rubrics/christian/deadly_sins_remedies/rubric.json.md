# Christian: Seven deadly sins and their remedies (Parson's Tale)

## abstinence

The Parson's Tale on abstinence, the remedy against gluttony, and its 'felawes'.

> Agayns Glotonye is the remedie Abstinence, as seith Galien; but that holde I nat meritorie, if he do it only for the hele of his body. Seint Augustin wole, that Abstinence be doon for vertu and with pacience. / Abstinence, he seith, is litel worth, but if a man have good wil ther-to, and but it be enforced by pacience and by charitee, and that men doon it for godes sake, and in hope to have the blisse of hevene.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §72

> The felawes of Abstinence been Attemperaunce, that holdeth the mene in alle thinges: eek Shame, that eschueth alle deshonestee: Suffisance, that seketh no riche metes ne drinkes ... Mesure also, that restreyneth by resoun the deslavee appetyt of etinge ... Sobrenesse also, that restreyneth the outrage of drinke

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §73

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Abstinence restrains eating and drinking. It is meritorious only when done for virtue, with patience and charity, for God's sake; abstaining only for health is not counted meritorious. Its companions are temperance that keeps the mean, sufficiency that seeks no rich food, measure that restrains appetite by reason, and sobriety in drink.

The companions are not separate items here. Fasting with impatience or pride is 'litel worth'; abstaining for health alone is not condemned, only not meritorious.

## avarice

The Parson's Tale on avarice and coveitise: the heart's greed for earthly things.

> Avarice, after the descripcion of seint Augustin, is likerousnesse in herte to have erthely thinges. / Som other folk seyn, that Avarice is, for to purchacen manye erthely thinges, and nothing yeve to hem that han nede. / And understond, that Avarice ne stant nat only in lond ne catel, but somtyme in science ... and in glorie

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §63

> Coveitise is for to coveite swiche thinges as thou hast nat; and Avarice is for to withholde and kepe swiche thinges as thou hast, with-oute rightful nede.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §63

> maketh that the avaricious man hath more hope in his catel than in Iesu Crist

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §63

> Of Coveitise comen thise harde lordshipes, thurgh whiche men been distreyned by tailages, custumes, and cariages, more than hir duetee or resoun is.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §64

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Avarice is greed for earthly things: buying up much and giving nothing to the needy. Coveting is wanting what one lacks; avarice proper is keeping what one has without rightful need. It can be for learning or fame as well as land and goods. The miser trusts his money more than Christ, and coveting breeds lords' exactions beyond what is due.

Keeping goods 'with rightful need' is not avarice; the Tale sets no amount. Its long passage on lords and serfs (§§64-67) is not summarised.

## chastity and continence

The Parson's Tale on chastity and continence, the remedy against lechery, in marriage and widowhood.

> Chastitee and Continence, that restreyneth alle the desordeynee moevinges that comen of fleshly talentes. ... And evere the gretter merite shal he han, that most restreyneth the wikkede eschaufinges of the ordure of this sinne. And this is in two maneres, that is to seyn, chastitee in mariage, and chastitee of widwehode.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §77

> she sholde loven hir housbonde with al hir herte, and to him be trewe of hir body / so sholde an housbonde eek be to his wyf. ... for three thinges a man and his wyf fleshly mowen assemble. The firste is in entente of engendrure of children to the service of god ... Another cause is, to yelden everich of hem to other the dette of hir bodies ... The thridde is, for to eschewe lecherye and vileinye. The ferthe is for sothe deadly sinne. / As to the firste, it is meritorie; the seconde also ... The thridde manere is venial sinne

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §80

> Another remedie agayns Lecherie is, specially to withdrawen swiche thinges as yeve occasion to thilke vileinye; as ese, etinge and drinkinge; for certes, whan the pot boyleth strongly, the beste remedie is to withdrawe the fyr.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §82

> Another remedie agayns Lecherie is, that a man or a womman eschue the companye of hem by whiche he douteth to be tempted; for al-be-it so that the dede is withstonden, yet is ther greet temptacioun.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §83

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Chastity and continence restrain the disordered stirrings of the flesh, and the merit grows with the restraint. They are kept in marriage, by faithfulness of body, and in widowhood. Marital union for children or to render the debt is meritorious; to avoid lechery it is venial; for burning delight alone it is deadly. Practical remedies are withdrawing occasions such as ease, food and drink, and avoiding company that tempts.

The Tale's marriage teaching includes a wife's subjection to her husband (§80); that historical claim is not used to judge chastity. Its ranking of virginity, widowhood and marriage concerns merit, not whether an act shows chastity.

## envy

The Parson's Tale on envy: sorrow at another's good and joy at his harm, with its branches.

> Envye, which is, as by the word of the philosophre, sorwe of other mannes prosperitee; and after the word of seint Augustin, it is sorwe of other mannes wele, and Ioye of othere mennes harm.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §30

> For wel unnethe is ther any sinne that it ne hath som delyt in itself, save only Envye, that evere hath in itself anguish and sorwe.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §30

On backbiting, which comes of envy:

> Som man preiseth his neighebore by a wikke entente; / for he maketh alwey a wikked knotte atte laste ende. ... The fifte spece is this; for to consente gladly and herkne gladly to the harm that men speke of other folk.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §30

> Thanne cometh eek bitternesse of herte; thurgh which bitternesse every good dede of his neighebor semeth to him bitter and unsavory.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §30

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Envy is sorrow at another's prosperity and joy at his harm; it has no pleasure of its own. It grows into backbiting (praise with a sting at the end, twisting good deeds, gladly hearing slander), grumbling, bitterness at a neighbour's good deeds, and secret malice.

Wanting a good one lacks, without sorrow at another's having it, is not shown to be envy. Grumbling has other roots too (pride, avarice, ire).

## fortitude

The Parson's Tale on Fortitudo or strength, the remedy against accidie (not the catechism's cardinal virtue).

> ther is a vertu that is called Fortitudo or Strengthe; that is, an affeccioun thurgh which a man despyseth anoyous thinges. / This vertu is so mighty and so vigorous, that it dar withstonde mightily and wysely kepen him-self fro perils that been wikked, and wrastle agayn the assautes of the devel.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §60

> For this Fortitudo may endure by long suffraunce the travailles that been covenable.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §60

Of magnanimity, its first species:

> ther bihoveth greet corage agains Accidie, lest that it ne swolwe the soule by the sinne of sorwe, or destroye it by wanhope. / This vertu maketh folk to undertake harde thinges and grevouse thinges, by hir owene wil, wysely and resonably.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §61

> Thanne is ther Constaunce, that is, stablenesse of corage; and this sholde been in herte by stedefast feith, and in mouth, and in beringe, and in chere and in dede.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §61

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Fortitude here is strength to despise wearisome things, to endure long the fitting labours, and to finish good works begun. Its species include great courage that takes on hard things willingly, wisely and reasonably, against sorrow and despair, and constancy. It also wisely keeps itself from wicked perils, so recklessness is not this virtue.

'Covenable' (fitting) bounds it: enduring needless or wicked toil is not shown to be this virtue. This is the remedy of sloth, not battlefield courage.

## gentleness

The Parson's Tale on Mansuetude or 'debonairetee', the first remedy against ire.

> The remedye agayns Ire is a vertu that men clepen Mansuetude, that is Debonairetee; and eek another vertu, that men callen Pacience or Suffrance.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §48

> Debonairetee withdraweth and refreyneth the stiringes and the moevynges of mannes corage in his herte, in swich manere that they ne skippe nat out by angre ne by Ire.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §49

> Seint Ierome seith thus of debonairetee, that ‘it doth noon harm to no wight, ne seith; ne for noon harm that men doon or seyn, he ne eschaufeth nat agayns, his resoun.’

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §49

Of good ire against wickedness:

> This Ire is with debonairetee, and it is wrooth withouten bitternesse

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §33

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Gentleness holds back the heart's stirrings so they do not break out in anger. It harms no one by word or deed and does not grow heated against reason when harmed. It may come from nature or be perfected by grace. It differs from patience, which bears wrongs; gentleness governs one's own impulses, and it can accompany good ire against a misdeed.

The Tale gives no worked case of gentleness alone; the contrast with patience comes from its pairing of the two remedies.

## gluttony

The Parson's Tale on gluttony: unmeasured appetite for food and drink, with its species.

> Glotonye is unmesurable appetyt to ete or to drinke, or elles to doon y-nogh to the unmesurable appetyt and desordeynce coveityse to eten or to drinke.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §70

> The firste is dronkenesse, that is the horrible sepulture of mannes resoun; and therfore, whan a man is dronken, he hath lost his resoun; and this is deedly sinne. / But soothly, whan that a man is nat wont to strong drinke, and peraventure ne knoweth nat the strengthe of the drinke, or hath feblesse in his heed, or hath travailed, thurgh which he drinketh the more, al be he sodeynly caught with drinke, it is no deedly sinne, but venial.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §70

Gregory's five species:

> The firste is, for to ete biforn tyme to ete. The seconde is, whan a man get him to delicat mete or drinke. / The thridde is, whan men taken to muche over mesure. The fourthe is curiositee, with greet entente to maken and apparaillen his mete. The fifthe is, for to eten to gredily.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §71

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Gluttony is unmeasured appetite for eating or drinking, or indulging it. Drunkenness buries reason and is deadly; but being caught unawares by strong drink through inexperience, weakness or fatigue is only venial. Gregory's species are eating before time, seeking delicacies, taking too much, fussing over dishes, and eating too greedily.

Venial drunkenness is still a fault, but less. Eating more for real bodily need is not 'unmesurable'.

## humility

The Parson's Tale on humility or meekness, the remedy against pride.

> the remedie agayns the sinne of pryde, and that is, humilitee or mekenesse. / That is a vertu, thurgh which a man hath verray knoweleche of him-self, and holdeth of him-self no prys ne deyntee as in regard of hise desertes, consideringe evere his freletee.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §29

> The humilitee in herte is in foure maneres: that oon is, whan a man holdeth him-self as noght worth biforn god of hevene. Another is, whan he ne despyseth noon other man. / The thridde is, whan he rekketh nat thogh men holde him noght worth. The ferthe is, whan he nis nat sory of his humiliacion.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §29

> Another is, whan he preiseth the bountee of another man, and nothing ther-of amenuseth. / Humilitee eek in werkes is in foure maneres: the firste is, whan he putteth othere men biforn him. The seconde is, to chese the loweste place over-al. The thridde is, gladly to assente to good conseil. / The ferthe is, to stonde gladly to the award of hise sovereyns, or of him that is in hyer degree

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §29

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Humility is true knowledge of oneself: claiming no worth from one's deserts and remembering one's frailty. In the heart it does not despise others and does not mind being thought little of. In speech it praises another's goodness without belittling it. In deeds it puts others first, takes the lowest place, and gladly accepts good counsel and the judgement of superiors.

The Tale asks assent to 'good' counsel; it does not require following bad counsel. Self-belittling that denies a real good is not clearly this virtue, which rests on 'verray knoweleche'.

## lechery

The Parson's Tale on lechery, forbidden in desire as well as deed, with its species.

> nat only that god forbad avoutrie in dede, but eek he comanded that thou sholdest nat coveite thy neighebores wyf. ... Here may ye seen that nat only the dede of this sinne is forboden, but eek the desyr to doon that sinne.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §75

> The firste finger is the fool lookinge of the fool womman and of the fool man ... The seconde finger is the vileyns touchinge in wikkede manere ... The thridde, is foule wordes ... The fourthe finger is the kissinge ... The fifthe finger of the develes hand is the stinkinge dede of Lecherie.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §76

> And for that many man weneth that he may nat sinne, for no likerousnesse that he doth with his wyf; certes, that opinion is fals.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §76

> Of Lecherie, as I seyde, sourden diverse speces; as fornicacioun, that is bitwixe man and womman that been nat maried; and this is deedly sinne

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §76

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Lechery is forbidden in desire as well as deed. Its approaches are lustful looking, wanton touching, foul words, kissing and the act itself. Its species include fornication between the unmarried, deflowering a virgin, adultery, sins by those vowed to chastity, lust within marriage sought only for pleasure, incest and sins against nature. Within marriage the Tale grades union: for children or to render the marital debt it is meritorious, to avoid lechery it is venial, and only for burning delight it is deadly.

The Tale recalls Old Law death penalties for adultery (§74) and Christ's pardon of the adulteress (§76); these are its historical claims, not measures of an act.

## love of God and neighbour

The Parson's Tale on the remedy against envy: love of God first, and of one's neighbour, including one's enemy, as oneself.

> First, is the love of god principal, and loving of his neighebor as him-self; for soothly, that oon ne may nat been withoute that other.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §31

> Thy neighebore artow holden for to love, and wilne him alle goodnesse ... And more-over, thou shalt love him in word, and in benigne amonestinge, and chastysinge; and conforten him in hise anoyes, and preye ... for him with al thyn herte. / And in dede thou shall love him in swich wyse, that thou shalt doon to him in charitee as thou woldest that it were doon to thyn owene persone.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §31

> Understond eek, that in the name of neighebor is comprehended his enemy.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §31

> Agayns hate and rancour of herte, he shal love him in herte. Agayns chyding and wikkede wordes, he shal preye for his enemy. And agayn the wikked dede of his enemy, he shal doon him bountee.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §31

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Love of God and of neighbour go together. Love wills the neighbour every good: in words, by kind admonition, correction, comfort and prayer; in deeds, by doing to him as one would have done to oneself and harming neither body, goods nor soul. The enemy is a neighbour too: answer his hatred with love, his insults with prayer, and his harm with good.

Love includes correction ('chastysinge'), so a fitting rebuke is not a failure of love. The Tale gives no separate account of love of God apart from love of neighbour.

## mercy and pity

The Parson's Tale on 'misericorde, and pitee largely taken', the relief of avarice.

> the relevinge of Avarice is misericorde, and pitee largely taken.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §68

> Certes, the avaricious man sheweth no pitee ne misericorde to the nedeful man; for he delyteth him in the kepinge of his tresor, and nat in the rescowinge ne relevinge of his evene-cristene.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §68

> misericorde, as seith the philosophre, a vertu, by which the corage of man is stired by the misese of him that is misesed. / Up-on which misericorde folweth pitee, in parfourninge of charitable werkes of misericorde.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §68

> The speces of misericorde been, as for to lene and for to yeve and to foryeven and relesse, and for to han pitee in herte, and compassioun of the meschief of his evene-cristene, and eek to chastyse there as nede is.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §68

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Mercy is being moved by another's distress; pity follows it in doing works of mercy. Its kinds are lending, giving, forgiving and releasing debts, inner compassion, and also correcting where needed. It relieves avarice because the miser delights in keeping his treasure instead of rescuing his neighbour.

Being moved without any help, when help was possible, shows less than the Tale's 'parfourninge'. The Tale does not say how much must be given.

## patience

Chaucer's Parson's Tale (Skeat, Middle English): patience as a remedy against Ire.

> The remedye agayns Ire is a vertu that men clepen Mansuetude, that is Debonairetee; and eek another vertu, that men callen Pacience or Suffrance. ... **Pacience, that is another remedye agayns Ire, is a vertu that suffreth swetely every mannes goodnesse, and is nat wrooth for noon harm that is doon to him.**

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §§48–50: remedies of ire

> The gode Ire is by Ialousye of goodnesse, thurgh which a man is wrooth with wikkednesse ... This Ire is with debonairetee, and it is wrooth withouten bitternesse; nat wrooth agayns the man, but wrooth with the misdede of the man

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §33: qualification concerning good and wicked ire

good ire is compatible with debonairetee, but is not itself proof of patience.

## pride

The Parson's Tale (Chaucer, c. 1390) on pride, the root of the seven deadly sins, with its 'twigs'.

> Of the roote of thise sevene sinnes thanne is Pryde, the general rote of alle harmes; for of this rote springen certein braunches, as Ire, Envye, Accidie or Slewthe, Avarice or Coveitise (to commune understondinge), Glotonye, and Lecherye.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §23

> Avauntour, is he that bosteth of the harm or of the bountee that he hath doon. / Ipocrite, is he that hydeth to shewe him swiche as he is, and sheweth him swiche as he noght is. / Despitous, is he that hath desdeyn of his neighebore, that is to seyn, of his evene-cristene, or hath despyt to doon that him oghte to do. ... Arrogant, is he that thinketh that he hath thilke bountees in him that he hath noght, or weneth that he sholde have hem by hise desertes ... Impacient, is he that wol nat been y-taught ne undernome of his vyce

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §24

> And yet is ther a privee spece of Pryde, that waiteth first to be salewed er he wole salewe ... and eek he waiteth or desyreth to sitte, or elles to goon above him in the wey ... but that he hath his herte and his entente in swich a proud desyr to be magnifyed and honoured biforn the peple.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §25

> The especes that sourden of pryde, soothly whan they sourden of malice ymagined, avysed, and forncast, or elles of usage, been deedly synnes, it is no doute. / And whan they sourden by freletee unavysed sodeinly, and sodeinly withdrawen ayein, al been they grevouse sinnes, I gesse that they ne been nat deedly.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §27

After condemning costly array:

> I speke this for the sinne of superfluitee, and nat for reasonable honestetee, whan reson it requyreth.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §27

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Pride is the root sin. Its twigs include boasting of harm or good done, hypocrisy (showing oneself as one is not), disdain of one's neighbour, arrogance (claiming goods one lacks, or deserving them), refusing correction, and craving to be greeted and seated first. Pride planned or habitual is deadly; a sudden lapse quickly withdrawn is grievous but not deadly. Seemly dress suited to one's state is not condemned.

The twigs overlap other sins (impatience, strife, vainglory); one twig is enough to show some pride.

## reasonable generosity

The Parson's Tale on 'resonable largesse', the second remedy against avarice, set against fool-largesse.

> Another manere of remedie agayns Avarice is resonable largesse; but soothly, here bihoveth the consideracioun of the grace of Iesu Crist, and of hise temporel goodes, and eek of the godes perdurables that Crist yaf to us

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §68

> and eek that he shal forgon al that he hath, save only that he hath despended in gode werkes.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §68

> men oghten eschue fool-largesse, that men clepen wast. / Certes, he that is fool-large ne yeveth nat his catel, but he leseth his catel. Soothly, what thing that he yeveth for veyne glorie, as to minstrals and to folk, for to beren his renoun in the world, he hath sinne ther-of and noon almesse.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §69

Among the signs of true gentility:

> to be liberal, that is to seyn, large by mesure; for thilke that passeth mesure is folye and sinne.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §28

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Reasonable largesse is giving by measure, mindful of what Christ gave and that one keeps only what is spent in good works. Its counterfeit is fool-largesse or waste: giving for vainglory and renown, or where one should not give. Such gifts are sin, not alms.

The Tale sets no amounts; 'by mesure' means fitted to need and to one's means.

## sloth

The Parson's Tale on accidie or sloth: sorrow and weariness toward good, with its branches.

> Thanne is Accidie the anguissh of a trouble herte; and seint Augustin seith: ‘it is anoy of goodnesse and Ioye of harm.’

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §53

> he dooth alle thing with anoy, and with wrawnesse, slaknesse, and excusacioun, and with ydelnesse and unlust

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §53

> it ne hath no purveaunce agayn temporel necessitee; for it forsleweth and forsluggeth, and destroyeth alle goodes temporeles by reccheleesnesse.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §53

> Now comth wanhope, that is despeir of the mercy of god

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §56

> And certes, the tyme that, by wey of resoun, men sholde nat slepe, that is by the morwe; but-if ther were cause resonable.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §56

> Thanne comth Lachesse; that is he, that whan he biginneth any good werk, anon he shal forleten it and stinten; as doon they that han any wight to governe, and ne taken of him na-more kepe, anon as they finden any contrarie or any anoy.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §59

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Sloth is weariness toward good: doing duties with reluctance, slackness, excuses and idleness, and neglecting even bodily provision. Its branches include refusing hardship, dread of beginning good work, despair of God's mercy, oversleeping without reason, negligence about doing things well, and dropping a good work or a charge at the first difficulty.

Sleep or rest with a reasonable cause is not condemned. The Tale places despair here; Cassian puts it under dejection, a different division.

## wrath

The Parson's Tale on ire: the wicked will to be avenged, set apart from good ire against wickedness.

> This sinne of Ire, after the discryving of seint Augustin, is wikked wil to been avenged by word or by dede.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §33

> The gode Ire is by Ialousye of goodnesse, thurgh which a man is wrooth with wikkednesse and agayns wikkednesse ... This Ire is with debonairetee, and it is wrooth withouten bitternesse; nat wrooth agayns the man, but wrooth with the misdede of the man

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §33

> wikked Ire is in two maneres, that is to seyn, sodeyn Ire or hastif Ire, withouten avisement and consentinge of resoun. / The mening and the sens of this is, that the resoun of man ne consente nat to thilke sodeyn Ire; and thanne it is venial. / Another Ire is ful wikked, that comth of felonye of herte avysed and cast biforn; with wikked wil to do vengeance, and therto his resoun consenteth; and soothly this is deedly sinne.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §33

> right so is rancour norice and keper of Ire.

Chaucer, Parson's Tale §33

The quotations are unmodernised Middle English; the explanation gives their plain sense. Wicked ire is the will to be avenged by word or deed. Sudden anger that reason does not consent to is venial; anger with a planned, consented will to vengeance is deadly, and rancour keeps it alive. Good ire, zeal against wickedness, is angry at the misdeed and not the man, without bitterness. It is not this sin.

Stories often leave unstated whether anger targets the deed or the man and whether it seeks vengeance; do not assume either.
