# Christian: Eight principal faults (Cassian)

## accidie

Cassian's sixth principal fault (acedia), weariness or distress of heart, as taught to monks: escaping one's appointed place and work by sleep or flight.

> Of accidie there are two kinds : one of which sends those affected by it to sleep; while the other makes them forsake their cell and flee away.

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> Our sixth combat is with what the Greeks call ... which we may term weariness or distress of heart. This is akin to dejection, and is especially trying to solitaries

Cassian, Institutes X.1

> It also makes the man lazy and sluggish about all manner of work which has to be done within the enclosure of his dormitory. It does not suffer him to stay in his cell, or to take any pains about reading ... Then the disease ... suggests that he ought to show courteous and friendly hospitalities to the brethren, and pay visits to the sick, whether near at hand or far off.

Cassian, Institutes X.2

> so that he may neither fall stricken through by the shaft of slumber, nor be driven out from the monastic cloister, even though under some pious excuse or pretext, and depart as a runaway.

Cassian, Institutes X.5

> You have not freed yourself from it, but rather have given yourself up to it as its slave and subject.

Cassian, Institutes X.25

Accidie is weariness with one's appointed place and work. It leads either to idle sleep or to restless flight, and often disguises flight as piety: visits, hospitality, care of relatives. The test is whether the good-sounding errand is a way of escaping the work. The cure is to stay and endure it, not to run.

Limits: The cell, reading and fixed hours are monastic; for others the analogue is one's assigned duty, which is an extension. Visiting the sick is good in itself; it counts here only when it is shown to be flight.

## anger

Cassian's fourth principal fault (ira), as Serapion and the Institutes teach it: anger against others in any form, inward, outward or lasting.

> Of anger there are three kinds: one which rages within ... another which breaks out in word and deed and action ... the third, which is not like those in boiling over and being done with in an hour, but which lasts for days and long periods ... And all these three must be condemned by us with equal horror.

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> he excepts none whatever as necessary or useful for us. And if need be, he should at once treat an erring brother in such a way that, while he manages to apply a remedy to one afflicted with perhaps a slight fever, he may not by his wrath involve himself in a more dangerous malady of blindness.

Cassian, Institutes VIII.5

> And some are commanded to "be angry" after a wholesome fashion, but with our own selves, and with evil thoughts that arise

Cassian, Institutes VIII.9

> they say in words that they are not angry, but in fact and deed they show that ... they are extremely disturbed? For they do not speak to them pleasantly, nor address them with ordinary civility, and they think that they are not doing wrong in this, because they do not seek to avenge themselves

Cassian, Institutes VIII.11

> that in case we are aware that they have anything against us, whether justly or unjustly, we should leave our gift, that is, postpone our prayers, and hasten first to offer satisfaction to them

Cassian, Institutes VIII.14

Cassian allows no anger at other people, even at a wrongdoer; an erring brother is to be corrected without wrath. Inward rage, an outburst, and anger nursed for days all count. A cold refusal of ordinary civility is anger, not its absence. The only anger he permits is against one's own faults and evil thoughts.

Limits: Stricter than texts that allow anger at wickedness, such as the Parson's Tale's 'good ire'; keep Cassian's line here. He writes for monks and does not discuss rulers, judges or defence.

## avarice

Cassian's third principal fault (philargyria, 'the love of money'), as taught to monks who renounce property.

> Of covetousness there are three kinds: (1) That which hinders ... renunciants from allowing themselves to be stripped of their goods and property; (2) that which draws us to resume with excessive eagerness the possession of those things which we have given away and distributed to the poor; (3) that which leads a man to covet and procure what he never previously possessed.

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> And so we must not only guard against the possession of money, but also must expel from our souls the desire for it. For we should not so much avoid the results of covetousness, as cut off by the roots all disposition towards it. For it will do no good not to possess money, if there exists in us the desire for getting it.

Cassian, Institutes VII.21

> For it is possible even for one who has no money to be by no means free from the malady of covetousness

Cassian, Institutes VII.22

> it begins by tempting him in regard of a small sum of money, giving him excellent and almost reasonable excuses why he ought to retain some money for himself.

Cassian, Institutes VII.7

> For it is plain that nothing belongs to the actual needs and necessities of our common life except our daily meat and drink

Cassian, Conferences V.8

Avarice is the love of money. Monks show it by keeping goods they renounced or taking back what they gave to the poor. Anyone shows it by coveting and procuring what they never had. It lies in desire more than in possession: a poor man can be covetous. It usually starts with plausible excuses about future need.

Limits: Cassian sets no lay standard for property, saving or trade; his cure is renunciation inside the monastery. For someone who has not renounced property, only the third kind applies.

## dejection

Cassian's fifth principal fault (tristitia, dejection), as taught to monks: worldly sorrow that sours prayer, work and dealings with others.

> Of dejection there are two kinds: one, that which springs up when anger has died down, or is the result of some loss we have incurred or of some purpose which has been hindered and interfered with ; the other, that which comes from unreasonable anxiety of mind or from despair.

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> It does not allow it to say its prayers with its usual gladness of heart, nor permit it to rely on the comfort of reading the sacred writings, nor suffer it to be quiet and gentle with the brethren; it makes it impatient and rough in all the duties of work and devotion

Cassian, Institutes IX.1

> dejection is only useful to us in one case, when we yield to it either in penitence for sin, or through being inflamed with the desire of perfection, or the contemplation of future blessedness.

Cassian, Institutes IX.10

> But that dejection and sorrow which "worketh repentance steadfast unto salvation " is obedient, civil, humble, kindly, gentle, and patient ... But the other kind is rough, impatient, hard, full of rancour and useless grief and penal despair

Cassian, Institutes IX.11

> if, ever joyful with an insight into things eternal and future, and continuing immovable, we are not depressed by present accidents, or over-elated by prosperity

Cassian, Institutes IX.13

Dejection is worldly sorrow at spent anger, loss or a thwarted plan, or groundless anxiety and despair. It shows in conduct: prayer and work go sour and one becomes rough with others. Sorrow for sin, or longing for perfection or the life to come, is not this fault; it stays gentle and patient. Despair that refuses amendment is its worst form.

Limits: Cassian makes no exception for mourning; he treats all worldly sorrow as to be resisted. Inner sorrow that leaves no mark on conduct is hard to establish from a story.

## fornication

Cassian's second principal fault (porneia), as Abbot Serapion teaches it to monks: unchastity in deed, in solitary uncleanness, and in the consenting heart.

> Of fornication there are three sorts: (1) that which is accomplished by sexual intercourse; (2) that which takes place without touching a woman, for which we read that Onan the son of the patriarch Judah was smitten by the Lord; and which is termed by Scripture uncleanness : of which the Apostle says : " But I say to the unmarried and to widows, that it is good for them if they abide even as I. But if they do not contain let them marry: for it is better to marry than to burn ; " (3) that which is conceived in heart and mind, of which the Lord says in the gospel : " Whosoever looketh on a woman to lust after her hath already committed adultery with her in his heart."

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> gluttony and fornication, though they exist in us naturally (for sometimes they spring up without any incitement from the mind, and simply at the motion and allurement of the flesh) yet if they are to be consummated, must find an external object, and thus take effect only through bodily acts.

Cassian, Conferences V.4

> For from the body the object and material which would allure it must be withdrawn, for fear lest the lust should endeavour to break out into act ; and before the mind we should no less carefully place diligent meditation on Scripture and watchful anxiety and the withdrawal into solitude, lest it should give birth to desire even in thought.

Cassian, Conferences V.4

> fornication, which springs from superfluity and gluttony as its root

Cassian, Conferences V.6

The fault is lust in the act, in solitary uncleanness, or consented to in the heart. A bodily stirring that the mind does not take up is not yet the third kind. For those who cannot contain, the cited Apostle points to marriage. The remedy joins bodily restraint, removing the occasion and guarding the thoughts.

Limits: Gibson's translation omits Cassian's own book on this fault (Institutes VI) and Conference XII; this guide rests on Conference V. Cassian addresses celibate monks; he does not discuss married life beyond the Apostle's counsel.

## gluttony

Cassian's first principal fault (gastrimargia), as Abbot Serapion and the Institutes teach it to monks: eating ruled by appetite rather than need.

> of gluttony there are three sorts: (i) that which drives a monk to eat before the proper and stated times ; (2) that which cares about filling the belly and gorging it with all kinds of food, and (3) that which is on the lookout for dainties and delicacies.

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> there is a difference of time, manner, and quality of the refreshment in proportion to the difference of condition of the body, the age, and sex: but that there is one and the same rule of restraint to everybody as regards continence of mind ... there is one aim and object of continence in the case of all of these, viz. : that no one may be overburdened beyond the measure of his appetite, by gluttony.

Cassian, Institutes V.5

> Bodily weakness is no hindrance to purity of heart, if only so much food is taken as the bodily weakness requires, and not what pleasure asks for.

Cassian, Institutes V.7

> a fast, although it is useful and advisable, is yet a free-will offering. But the exigencies of a command require the fulfilment of a work of charity.

Cassian, Institutes V.24

Gluttony is eating for appetite and pleasure rather than need: breaking a set fast early, gorging, or seeking delicacies. Need differs with health, age and sex, so the weak may rightly eat more. Breaking a fast for charity, such as eating with guests, is not gluttony.

Limits: Cassian writes for monks under a fasting rule; the first kind presupposes such a rule. He also faults excessive fasting (Institutes V.9), but that is not gluttony.

## pride

Cassian's eighth principal fault (superbia), as the Institutes teach it: elation that claims one's goods as one's own, before God and toward men.

> And of this pride there are two kinds: the one, that by which we said that the best of men and spiritually minded ones were troubled; the other, that which assaults even beginners and carnal persons. And though each kind of pride is excited with regard to both God and man by a dangerous elation, yet that first kind more particularly has to do with God; the second refers especially to men.

Cassian, Institutes XII.2

> he believed that he had acquired the splendour of that wisdom and the beauty of those powers, with which he was graced by the gift of the Creator, by the might of his own nature, and not by the beneficence of His generosity. And on this account he was puffed up as if he stood in no need of divine assistance

Cassian, Institutes XII.4

> He is utterly lacking in patience, and without charity : impudent in offering insults to others, faint-hearted in bearing them himself: troublesome in the matter of obedience except where his own wishes and likings correspond with his duty: unforgiving in receiving admonition ... and thus the result is that though he is incapable of giving sound advice, yet in everything he prefers his own opinion to that of the elders.

Cassian, Institutes XII.29

> into these two we are in danger of falling when we have proved victorious, and above all after some splendid triumph.

Cassian, Conferences V.10

> And so we can escape the snare of this most evil spirit, if in the case of every virtue in which we feel that we make progress, we say these words of the Apostle: "Not I, but the grace of God with me,"

Cassian, Institutes XII.9

Pride claims one's goods as one's own achievement. Its spiritual form credits one's virtue to oneself rather than to God's grace. Its carnal form, toward men, shows in insulting others, refusing correction, and insisting on one's own will and opinion. It strikes after success. Its cure is to ascribe progress to grace and to bear slights patiently.

Limits: The spiritual kind is defined in relation to grace, so stories without a religious frame mostly show the carnal kind. Holding a true judgement firmly, without contempt or refusal of correction, is not shown to be pride by this text.

## vainglory

Cassian's seventh principal fault (cenodoxia), boasting or vain glory: seeking men's praise for visible or spiritual things.

> Of vainglory, although it takes various forms and shapes, and is divided into different classes, yet there are two main kinds: (1) when we are puffed up about carnal things and things visible, and (2) when we are inflamed with the desire of vain praise for things spiritual and unseen.

Cassian, Conferences V.11 (Abbot Serapion), tr. Gibson 1894

> it tries to injure the soldier of Christ in his dress, in his manner, his walk, his voice, his work, his vigils, his fasts, his prayers, when he withdraws, when he reads, in his knowledge, his silence, his obedience, his humility, his patience

Cassian, Institutes XI.3

> If a man fasts openly, he is attacked by the pride of vanity. If he conceals it for the sake of despising the glory of it, he is assailed by the same sin of pride.

Cassian, Institutes XI.4

> To begin with we should not allow ourselves to do anything at the suggestion of vanity, and for the sake of obtaining vainglory.

Cassian, Institutes XI.19

> we have wronged God by doing for the favour of men what we ought to have done for His sake

Cassian, Institutes XI.19

> it is better for a man to be troubled by the sin of vainglory than for him to fall into the desire for fornication

Cassian, Conferences V.12

Vainglory is doing or showing things to win men's praise, for visible goods like dress and looks or spiritual ones like fasts, prayers and learning. Because it rides on good deeds, the test is the motive of being seen, not the deed. It can hide even in showy humility. Cassian grants it may hold a beginner back from a worse sin, but it stays a fault.

Limits: Many of its signs are monastic (vigils, fasts, desire for holy orders). A good deed that happens to be seen, with no sign of seeking praise, is not vainglory.
