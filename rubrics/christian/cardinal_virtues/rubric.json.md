# Christian: Four cardinal virtues (Penny Catechism)

## fortitude

Cardinal virtue (also a gift of the Holy Ghost): courage against difficulties of salvation; bearing the cross.

> **It enables us to face with courage, and generously overcome, all the difficulties with which our salvation is beset, and to bear our cross patiently after our Blessed Lord.**

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III (1877), 15th instruction.

> It is this which will enable us to fulfil our daily duties faithfully, even when they are distasteful and painful to us.

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III (1877), 15th instruction.

## justice

Gibson (1877 Catholic catechist): cardinal justice is the habitual desire to give every one his due. It embraces all duties to God, neighbour and self, including piety, obedience, honesty and truthfulness.

> **The second Cardinal Virtue is Justice, which is the habitual desire to give to every one his due.** You see that, as a Cardinal Virtue, Justice has a wider meaning than we commonly give it ; for what we usually mean by being just is perfectly honest in our dealings with our [neighbour]; whereas a just man, in the sense of one who possesses this Cardinal Virtue, is a man who fulfils all his duties, whether to God, to his neighbour, or to himself.

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III (1877), 15th instruction

> You see then that Justice embraces many virtues, for example, piety and the fulfilment of all our religious duties, obedience to our parents and superiors, honesty, truthfulness, &c.

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III (1877), 15th instruction

## prudence

Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy vol. 3, pp. 230-232 (OCR preserved): one 19th-century Catholic catechism, not all Christian teaching.

> After the Theological, my dear children, come the Cardinal Virtues, which are these four — Prudence, Justice, Fortitude and Temperance. These virtues are sometimes called moral virtues, because they form our morals, or manners, aright, so as to enable us to fulfil properly all our duties. Considered as mere natural good qualities, or, as we sometimes say, human virtues, they existed even among pagans ; hence you read in Ancient History of " the Prudence of Cincinnatus," "the Justice of Aristides," &c. Here, of course, we consider them as Christian Virtues, that is, as supernatural habits infused into the soul by God, which enable us to form our daily lives according to his holy Law.

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III, pp. 230-231

> First of the four comes Prudence, which is that supernatural habit of the soul which enables us to see and take the proper means to arrive at the possession of God. Of course, I am not speaking of worldly prudence, which is often true folly and cannot justly be called a virtue, but of that heavenly prudence which guides us, and directs our actions aright, so that we may avoid the dangers which beset us, and preserve the straight road which leads to eternal life. Thus a prudent man will never act with rashness, but will carefully consider each step that he takes in his passage through life, and in matters of doubt will be guided by the advice of the wise and virtuous. In all things he will consult his spiritual rather than his temporal interests, and will readily sacrifice the false and fleeting goods of this world to secure the true and everlasting riches of the next.

Gibson, Catechism Made Easy III, p. 231

Gibson sets apart worldly prudence aimed only at temporal ends, but he recognizes natural prudence among pagans. Taking ordinary temporal interests into account is therefore not itself corrupt.

## temperance

Cardinal virtue: moderation in all appetites and desires, even in good and devout actions.

> **The last of the Cardinal Virtues is Temperance, by which we mean a moderation in all things, not merely in eating and drinking, but in the gratification of all our appetites and desires.** ... For example, the mother of a family, who is devout and fond of prayer, might without the virtue of Temperance or Moderation fall into a sin of excess by giving her whole time to that exercise, and thus neglecting the care of her family. ... But it is not often that we exceed in what is good; and it is chiefly in regard to those things which indulge our self-love, such as eating, drinking, sleeping, and the different pleasures of life, that we stand in need of the safeguard of Temperance.

Henry Gibson, Catechism Made Easy, vol.III (1877)

Broader than the capital-sin Temperance item, which covers food and drink only. Gibson's illustrations are examples, not exhaustive cases.
