# Christian: Eight Beatitudes (Penny Catechism)

## the clean of heart

Gibson: purity and innocence, dread of sin, guarded senses, watchfulness and prayer against temptation.

> **The clean of heart, my dear children, are those who are pure and innocent, who tremble at the thought of sin, who carefully guard their senses and ... ever watch and pray against temptation.** Such was St. Aloysius, who would not so much as fix his eyes on a person of the other sex; such too was St. Stanislaus of Kostka, whose presence was sufficient to restrain among his schoolmates every unbecoming word.

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

The saints are examples, not templates. The incidental details of Aloysius's conduct are not requirements.

Limits: Gibson emphasizes sexual purity but frames the quality as innocence generally. Do not require a monastic or saintly profile.

## the meek

Gibson: on receiving injury, restraining revenge and returning good for evil.

> **Meekness, my dear children, is that virtue which enables us, on receiving an injury, to restrain all desire of revenge.** ... Those, therefore, will inherit this beatitude who, imitating the example of our Blessed Lord, when they are reviled do not revile, and when they suffer threaten not ... who render not evil for evil, but good for evil ... who love their enemies, do good to those that hate them, and ... pray for those who persecute and calumniate them.

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

The quality concerns the response to injury. Mere quietness or lack of assertiveness does not count.

Limits: The source's claims about the earthly success of the meek are promises, not criteria. Do not require that an enemy be converted.

## the merciful

Gibson: showing mercy and compassion through works of mercy, such as feeding the hungry.

> 5. Blessed are the merciful; for they shall obtain mercy — in other words, **those who show mercy and compassion to their fellow men shall meet with the same themselves from Almighty God.** ... " come, ye blessed of my Father, and possess ye the kingdom prepared for you; for I was hungry and you gave me to eat," &c. On the other hand, he will say to the wicked, " Depart from me you cursed into everlasting fire." And why? Because you have not exercised those same works of mercy during life, which would have obtained for you before death the grace of true repentance and the, Divine pardon.

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

Limits: The conversion that followed the alms is a reported teaching story, not verified. Mercy here does not require the giver to be sinless.

## the peace-makers

Gibson: promoting twofold peace, between God and the sinner and among people.

> Now peace is of two kinds. It was to establish peace between God and man that our Blessed Lord came upon earth and died on the cross ... But he wishes us to have peace not only with God, but also with one another. ... **Whoever, therefore, promotes this twofold peace by converting the sinner to God, and by healing or preventing quarrels and ... dissensions among men, inherits this blessing**

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

A single episode may show either kind of peace. It need not show both.

## the poor in spirit

Gibson: detachment of heart from riches, whether one is rich (stewardship) or poor (resignation).

> 1. Blessed are the poor in spirit, that is, those who are poor in heart and affection; for the spirit of the world is to love and make a God of riches, but the spirit of Jesus Christ is to set no esteem on worldly wealth, but only on the treasure of God's grace and friendship. **Those, then, are poor in spirit who, if rich, do not set their heart on riches, but use them only as the stewards of God to advance his glory and promote the welfare of his creatures. Those, also, are poor in spirit who, if placed by God in a state of poverty, are resigned to that state, and content with the position which God has assigned to them.**

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

Gibson locates this quality in the heart's affection, not in the amount a person owns.

Limits: Poverty by itself is not the quality.

## they that hunger and thirst after justice

Gibson: ardent desire and earnest striving to become good, fulfilling duties to God, neighbor, and self.

> Justice, as we have already seen, is a cardinal virtue, including many others — in fact, all the virtues which enable us to fulfil our duty to God, our neighbour, and our own souls. **He, then, hungers and thirsts after justice who ardently desires and earnestly strives to become good.** Such a disposition is a sure sign that the soul will arrive at great perfection; for God helps those abundantly whom he sees to have a good will to love and serve him.

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

The quality is the desire and the striving, not completed sanctity and not a thirst for vengeance.

Limits: The source refers back to an earlier treatment of justice that is not supplied. Do not fill that gap from memory.

## they that mourn

Gibson: patient affliction for God's love, repentance for one's sins, compassion for others' sorrows.

> Because the pleasures and enjoyments of this world are short and fleeting, nor can they ever satisfy the heart of man; while, on the other hand, **afflictions borne patiently for the love of God, tears of repentance for our own sins, and those of tender compassion for the sorrows of others, merit for us the eternal joys and delights of paradise.**

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

Not all sadness qualifies. Any one of the three named forms can be enough.

## they that suffer persecution for justice sake

Gibson: suffering for religion or virtue, borne as a happiness for Jesus' name.

> 8. Finally, Blessed are they who suffer persecution for justice sake, **that is, in the cause of religion or virtue.** ... But it is not the martyrs only who share in this beatitude ... Every good man has something to endure from the calumny, violence, or injustice of others. Let him, then, whatever he suffers, whatever sacrifices he has to make, esteem it, as the Apostles did, a great happiness to suffer something for the name of Jesus.

Gibson, Catechism Made EasyIII1877, Eight Beatitudes pp251–258

Suffering alone, or punishment for a real wrong, is not this quality.

Limits: The later vindication in the Vincent story is a reported outcome, not a criterion. No explicit Christian wording is required in ordinary virtue cases.
