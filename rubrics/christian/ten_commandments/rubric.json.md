# Christian: Ten Commandments

## adultery

Golden Legend: the outward deed of adultery, extended to all lechery and sins of the flesh.

> thou shalt not do adultery, that is to say, thou shalt not have fleshly company with another man’s wife. **In this commandment it is forbidden and defended [forbidden] all manner sin of the flesh which is called generally lechery** ... How be it that there is some branch of it that is not deadly sin, as off movings of the flesh that may not be eschewed, which men ought to restrain and refrain as much as they may.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: Unavoidable movings are not deadly sin, yet they are to be restrained. The source's wording is historically male-framed. Outward deed belongs here; inner consent belongs to the ninth commandment.

## coveting a neighbour's goods

Golden Legend: consented will to have others' goods wrongfully, and envy of others' weal.

> This commandment defendeth [forbids] will to have things that belong to other men by evil reason or wrongfully. In this commandment is defended [forbidden] envy of other men’s weal, of other men’s grace or welfare. ... **And this covetise is when the consenting and ... thought be certainly one, then it is deadly sin. And if there be any evil movings without will and consenting of damage or hurt of other, this is not deadly sin. If he sin herein it is but venial sin.**

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: 'Not deadly' is not the same as no breach; the source's wording on venial sin is conditional. Lawful wanting is not condemned.

## desiring a neighbour's wife

Golden Legend: inner consent to unlawful union, and outward signs that draw toward that sin.

> nor shalt not covet her in thine heart, that is to say, thou shalt not consent to sin with her with thy body ... **the sixth commandment forbiddeth the deed without forth, and this forbiddeth the consenting within forth** ... This is to understand of the consenting expressed in his thought.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> This commandment defendeth [forbids] to desire to have company with all manner women out of marriage, and the evil signs that be without forth make men for to draw them to sin, as the evil words of such matter, or the foul and evil attouching, kissing, handling and such other.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: This commandment concerns inner consent; the outward deed belongs to the sixth. The wording is historically male-framed.

## false witness

Golden Legend: knowing lies, perjury, and malicious speech or detraction that impairs reputation, not only in courts.

> **In this commandment is forbidden that no man shall lie wittingly, for whoso lieth doth against this commandment.** And also that he forswear not him in judgment, ne make no leasings [lies] to annoy ne grieve another, nor he ought not to missay ne speak evil of others in intention to impair his good name and fame ... Against this commandment do they that say evil of good men behind them, and backbite them, and do this wittingly by malice, which is called detraction.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: The commandment extends beyond court testimony. It concerns knowing falsehood and malicious intent, not honest error.

## honouring father and mother

Golden Legend: honour toward parents and spiritual fathers, including obedience when they teach required good.

> **This commandment admonisheth us that we be well ware to anger father and mother in any wise.** Or who that curseth them or set hand on them in evil will, sinneth deadly.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> In this commandment is understood the honour that we should do to our ghostly and spiritual fathers, that is to them that have the cure of us, to teach and chastise us ... **And he that will not obey to him that hath the cure over him when he enseigneth [teaches] and teacheth him good that he is bound to do, he sinneth grievously and is inobedient**

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: The obedience clause covers teaching of good one is bound to do. Obeying a command to sin is not this honour, and the source gives no ruling on such cases.

## keeping holy the Sabbath or Sunday

Golden Legend: Sunday and holy-day rest that is turned toward prayer and God's service.

> **That is to say, that thou shalt do no work nor operation on the Sunday or holy day, but thou shalt rest from all worldly labour and intend to prayer, and to serve God thy maker** ... This commandment accomplisheth he that keepeth to his power the peace of his conscience for to serve God more holily.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> they that break the Sunday and the other solemn feasts ... they sin deadly ... **but if it be for some necessity that holy church admitteth and granteth.** But they sin much more then, that employ the Sunday and the feasts in sins, in lechery, in going to taverns in the service time, in gluttony and drinking drunk ... For then be they drunk, fight and slay

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Rest alone is not the virtue. The exposition joins rest from labour to prayer, thanksgiving and ghostly works.

Limits: Work for 'necessity that holy church admitteth' is excepted. The source does not list which necessities qualify.

## killing

Golden Legend: slaying for evil cause, and willful hate, rancour or revenge. Lawful justice is excepted.

> no man shall slay the other for vengeance, ne for his goods, or for any other evil cause, it is deadly sin. **But for to slay malefactors in executing of justice or for other good cause, if it be lawful it may well be done.** In this commandment is defended [forbidden] the sin of wrath and hate, of rancour and of ire.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> And yet sinneth he more that doth or purchaseth shame, villany or hurt to another wrongfully, or counselleth or helpeth to grieve another for to avenge him. **But wrath or anger lightly past without will to noy [annoy] or grieve any other, is not deadly sin.**

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: Lawful execution of justice is excepted, not praised as resistance. The source gives no ruling on suicide. Passing anger is not equated with willful harm.

## taking God's name in vain

Golden Legend: vain, false, light or blasphemous swearing. Rightful oaths are allowed.

> **But for good cause and rightful a man may swear without sin, as in judgment or in requiring of truth, or without judgment in good and needful causes.** ... If he swear false wittingly he is forsworn, and that is against the commandment and sinneth deadly

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> But to swear lightly without hurt or blame is venial sin, but the custom thereof is perilous and may well turn to deadly sin but if he take heed. But he then that sweareth horribly by our Lord, or by any of his members, or by his saints in despite, and blasphemeth ... he sinneth deadly

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: Not every oath is vain. Venial does not mean 'no breach'. Degree concerns gravity, not the strength of the evidence.

## theft

Golden Legend: wrongful taking, deceit or usury, and wrongful retention without restitution when able.

> to take away other men’s things whatsomever they be, without reason, against the will of them that owe [own] or make them. In this commandment is defended [forbidden] ravin, usury, robbery and deceit, and beguiling other for to have their havoir [possessions] or good.

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> **For who retaineth wrongfully and without reason other men’s goods against their will, sinneth deadly, if he pay not where as he oweth, if he know where and be in his power and hath whereof.** And if he know not let him do by the counsel of holy church

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Full passage:
Sometime around the turn of the first century BC, as the philosophies of Epicurus enjoyed a resurgence in Athens ... Diotimus [the Stoic philosopher] sat down and forged more than fifty “licentious letters” intended to slander the reputation of the founder of that rival school. ... Diotimus portrayed Epicurus as some kind of depraved maniac ... in order to bolster his arguments against the philosophy.

Part of the motivation was no doubt self-defense. The Epicurean school at this time was ascendant, and under the leadership of the prolific Apollodorus, who ... was nicknamed “the Garden Tyrant.” We are told by Diogenes Laërtius that Apollodorus had taken to smearing Chrysippus, claiming that the Stoic had filled his books with quotes he had stolen from others. Such slander of the Stoa’s great fighter would need to be addressed.

Diotimus chose to respond to slander with slander. He decided to commit a crime worse than what Apollodorus was falsely alleging against Chrysippus.

For a school that prized logic and truth as much as virtuous behavior, Diotimus’s actions would have been inexcusable. Even if Epicureanism was now posing some kind of existential threat to Stoicism, it hardly justifies the commission of literary fraud. ...

Athenaeus, citing Demetrius of Magnesia, says that Zeno of Sidon, who succeeded Apollodorus as head of the Epicurean school, tracked Diotimus down and filed suit against him. The court sided with Zeno of Sidon and sentenced Diotimus to death, which is a rather extreme form of justice—and certainly not one Rome would have countenanced.

While it’s unlikely that the death penalty would have been given for something as common as slander, there can be no doubt that a strong fine and exile from Athens were imposed. And greater than that, a personal shame.

## worshipping other gods

Golden Legend: idol worship, or principal hope or love set on creatures or treasure instead of God.

> That is to say, thou shalt worship no god but me, and thou shalt not retain thine hope but in me, **for who that setteth principally his hope on any creature or faith or belief in any thing more than in me, sinneth deadly.** And such be they that worship idols, and make their god of a creature

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

> And so do they that overmuch love their treasures, gold or silver, or any other earthly thing ... or set their heart or hope on any thing by which they forget and leave God their creator ... And therefore ought they to serve him with all their goods, and above all things to love him

Golden Legend, Caxton1483, ed.Ellis1900, vol.I Ten Commandments pp140–143

Limits: Possessions alone are neither the vice nor resistance to it. The fault lies in principal hope, or love that forgets God.
