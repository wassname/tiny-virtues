# Hindu: The five niyamas (Yoga Sūtra 2.32), in Vivekananda’s teaching

## cleanliness

Raja Yoga (in-brief section freely from Kurma Purana) and Vivekananda: external and internal purity, both needed.

> The sages have said that there are two sorts of purification, external and internal. The purification for the body is by water, earth, or other materials; the external purification, as by bathing, etc. Purification of the mind by truth, and by all the other virtues, is what is called internal purification. **Both are necessary.** It is not sufficient that a man should be internally pure and externally dirty. **When both are not attainable the internal purity is the better, but no one will be a Yogi until he has both.**

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899); ch. VIII, “Raja Yoga in Brief,” a summary freely translated from the Kurma Purana

> External purification is keeping the body pure; a dirty man will never be a Yogi. There must be internal purification also. ... Of course internal purity is of greater value than external, but both are necessary, and **external purity, without internal, is of no good.**

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899); Yoga Aphorisms II.32–34 and commentary

Limits: Ordinary hygiene alone isn't full śauca here; the in-brief section is a free Kurma Purana rendering within Vivekananda's book.

## contentment

Central Hindu College 1904 textbook gloss (not Vivekananda): happiness rooted in oneself, satisfaction with one's due.

> **The contented man is happy under the most unfavourable circumstances, the root of his happiness being in himself;** whereas the discontented man finds food for his discontent, however favourable his circumstances may be. There are always some who are superior in position to, more wealthy, more fortunate than ourselves, and hence reasons for discontent may ever be found by the unwise. To be satisfied with what we have because we have our due is true wisdom, and all dissatisfaction is folly.

Central Hindu College, Sanatana Dharma: Advanced Text Book (1904)

Vivekananda's supplied units only list contentment among niyamas; the definition comes from the CHC textbook.

Limits: CHC attribution only. Source doesn't address resisting injustice; don't read contentment as passivity toward harm, nor deny it.

## mortification

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899), in two layers. The commentary defines mortification as controlling body and mind, forbids extremes and flesh-torture, and treats powers as secondary to Yoga proper. Raja Yoga in Brief, freely translated from the Kurma Purana, names fasting as physical tapas.

> **Fasting, or in other ways controlling the body, is called the physical Tapas .**

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899); ch. VIII, “Raja Yoga in Brief,” a summary freely translated from the Kurma Purana

> What is meant, therefore, by this mortification? **Holding the reins firmly while guiding this body and mind; not letting the body do anything it likes, but keeping them both in proper control.**

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899)

> **A Yogi must avoid the two extremes of luxury and austerity. He must not fast, or torture his flesh;** he who does so, says the Gita, cannot be a Yogi; he who fasts; he who keeps awake; he who sleeps much; he who works too much; he who does no work; none of these can be Yogis.

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899)

> ... You will find men standing with their hands up all their lives, until their hands wither and die. ... Through such practices powers (Siddhis) can be attained. Concentration. **The concentration is Samadhi ’, and that is Yoga proper;** ... The preceding ones are only secondary, and we cannot attain to the highest through them.

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899); Yoga Aphorisms II.43 and commentary

Unresolved tension: Raja Yoga in Brief names fasting as physical tapas; the introduction forbids fasting. Don't choose one.

Limits: Not secular self-improvement or mere dieting. The fasting tension between Raja Yoga in Brief and the moderation passage stays unresolved. Powers are not moral virtue or Yoga proper. No claim is made about all Hindu schools.

## self-surrender to God

Vivekananda: worship and devotion to God; surrendering fruits of work, credit and blame, to the Lord.

> **Worship is by praise, by memory, having devotion to God.**

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899); ch. VIII, “Raja Yoga in Brief,” a summary freely translated from the Kurma Purana

> **“ By surrendering the fruits of work to God ” is to take to ourselves neither credit nor blame, but to give both up to the Lord, and be at peace.**

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899)

Limits: No requirement of specific religious vocabulary beyond devotion to God; obedience to humans is not this niyama.

## study

Vivekananda: mantra repetition and liberation-directed texts, intensifying conviction after prior inquiry.

> **Repeating the Vedas , and other Mantrams , by which the Sattva material in the body is purified, is called study, Svadhyaya .** There are three sorts of repetitions of these Mantrams . One is called the verbal, another semi-verbal, and the third mental. The verbal or audible is the lowest, and the inaudible is the highest

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899); ch. VIII, “Raja Yoga in Brief,” a summary freely translated from the Kurma Purana

> Study. What is meant by study in this case? **Not study of novels, or fiction, or story books, but study of those books which teach the liberation of the soul,** ... Then again this study does not mean controversial studies at all. ... **All these intellectual gymnastics are necessary at first. We must not go blindly into anything.** The Yogi has passed the argumentative stage, and has come to a conclusion, which is like the rocks, immovable. The only thing he now seeks to do is to intensify that conclusion. Do not argue, he says; if one forces arguments upon you, be silent.

Vivekananda, Raja Yoga (1899)

Limits: Not ordinary scholarship; novels-reading is mere absence, not conflict. Silence rule is stage-qualified, not anti-inquiry.
