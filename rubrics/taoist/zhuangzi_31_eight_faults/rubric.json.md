# Taoist: Zhuangzi's eight faults (八疵, ch. 31)

## Calumny

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.).

> … To delight in talking about other men’s failings is called calumny. …

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: The marker is delight, not falsehood. Watson’s “calumny” should not be read as requiring slander.

## Flattery

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.).

> … To speak without regard for what is right or wrong is called flattery. …

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: The fault is speech unmoored from right and wrong, not praise as such. It overlaps with treachery’s “without thought for right or wrong.”

## Maliciousness

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.).

> … To break up friendships and set kinfolk at odds is called maliciousness. … These eight faults inflict chaos on others and injury on the possessor.

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: The fault requires an agent who divides people. The source gives no motive requirement and no exceptions.

## Obsequiousness

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.): the stranger’s definition, spoken to Confucius.

> … To rush forward when no one has nodded in your direction is called obsequiousness. … These eight faults inflict chaos on others and injury on the possessor.

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: The source gives only a one-sentence definition, and gives no reason for the nod requirement. Ordinary deference is not resistance.

## Officiousness

The fisherman’s lesson to Confucius in Zhuangzi 31 (Watson tr., disputed chapter): eight faults, with role context.

> “Moreover, there are eight faults that men may possess … To do what it is not your business to do is called officiousness. … These eight faults inflict chaos on others and injury on the possessor.

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

> When officials attend to their duties and men worry about their undertakings, there is no overstepping of the mark. … Now on the higher level, you do not hold the position of a ruler, a feudal lord, or a chancellor, and on the lower level, you have not been assigned to the office of a high minister with its tasks and duties. Yet you presume to ‘bring a beautiful order to rites and music, … ’ This is undertaking rather a lot, isn’t it?

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: The fault turns on business that is not one’s own. Helping when one is asked, or acting within one’s role, is not evidence of it.

## Sycophancy

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.): a fault of speech and service.

> … To echo a man’s opinions and try to draw him out in speech is called sycophancy. …

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: Agreement alone is not this fault. The source does not explain why the echoer draws the other out.

## Treachery

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.): two-faced probing, not every betrayal.

> … Without thought for right or wrong, to try to face in two directions at once so as to steal a glimpse of the other party’s wishes is called treachery. These eight faults inflict chaos on others and injury on the possessor.

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: Watson’s “treachery” is technical. It overlaps with flattery’s disregard of right and wrong.

## Wickedness

Zhuangzi 31 eight faults (Watson tr.): a defined term, not any immoral act.

> … To praise falsely and hypocritically so as to cause injury and evil to others is called wickedness. …

Zhuangzi 31 The Old Fisherman, trans. Burton Watson 2013, stranger speaking

Limits: The term is narrow, unlike generic English “wickedness.” Harm done without false praise belongs elsewhere.
