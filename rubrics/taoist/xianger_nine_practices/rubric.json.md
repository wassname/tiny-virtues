# Taoist: The nine practices of the Xiang'er precepts (想爾九行)

## Practice clarity and stillness

Calming emotion and thought so that the body's pneumas circulate: a specific morning-and-evening practice, extended to not being swept along by passions or by the crowd.

The fifth of the Nine Practices (行清靜):

> Practice clarity and stillness.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> Of the "Nine Practices," "clarity and stillness," as we saw earlier, refers to a specific form of pneuma ingestion.

Bokenkamp, introduction to the Xiang'er (1997), p. 51

> Passion, worry, anger, joy, and evil are all things that the Dao does not desire. Your heart should regulate these emotions and finally stop them, causing them to scatter as ice melts under the sun.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 15, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 98

> When those who seek long life are given something, they do not decline it; when something is taken from them, they have no rancor. They do not follow the common run of people in their shifts and turns. Instead, their thoughts are perfectly directed toward the Dao. … Through constantly striving after clarity and stillness, the dews of morning and evening will ascend and descend [within them] and the pneumas of the human body will consequently be uniformly distributed

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 15, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), pp. 99–100

> 道人當自重精神，清靜為本。… 天子王公也，雖有榮觀，為人所尊，務當重清靜，奉行道誡也。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 26 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'People of the Dao should value their essence and spirit, taking clarity and stillness as the root. … This means the Son of Heaven, kings and lords: though they have glorious sights and are honoured by others, they must value clarity and stillness and carry out the precepts of the Dao.'

Clarity and stillness is first a cultivation practice, a daily calming so that pneuma circulates. The commentary ties it to conduct: emotions are regulated until they melt, gains and losses are met without rancour, and one does not drift with the crowd. Even rulers are told to put stillness first.

The meditative and physiological core cannot be seen in most single acts; conduct is evidence of it, not the practice itself. Calm is not indifference to others' suffering. Overlaps desirelessness. ## Practice desirelessness

Freedom from 'deviant' worldly desires, for wealth, rank, glory and fine things, rather than from all wanting: the adept still desires life and the Dao.

The seventh of the Nine Practices (行無欲):

> Practice desirelessness.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> Number seven of the "Nine Practices" enjoins one only to "practice desirelessness," but this requirement is clearly mediated by numbers five, eleven, twelve, fourteen, seventeen, and nineteen of the Xiang'er precepts, which mandate against what the text considers "deviant" or human desires for wealth, position, worldly glory, and the like.

Bokenkamp, introduction to the Xiang'er (1997), p. 55

> Not seeing that which is desirable will make your heart unruffled. … Not desiring to see something is like not seeing it at all. Do not allow your heart to be moved. If it is moved, restrain it.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 3, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 78

> One should practice the Dao to gain life, not to gain material goods. Material goods lead to artifice; thus, obtaining them will obstruct the Dao.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 12, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 93

> 道之所說，無私少欲於世俗耳。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 19 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'What the Dao speaks of is only being without selfishness and having few desires toward worldly things.' (on Laozi 19, 'lessen selfishness and reduce desires')

Desirelessness targets worldly desire for precious goods, wealth, rank, glory, fine clothes and food, and leaves the adept's longing for life and the Dao intact. In conduct it shows as not coveting, and as checking the heart when it stirs.

The text openly keeps a desire for long life and heavenly reward, so this is not total desirelessness. Meeting basic needs is not condemned; precept 19 forbids only strenuous seeking after wealth. ## Practice flexibility and weakness

Being supple and weak like water: going to the low place, giving way instead of forcing, and benefiting others without struggle.

The second of the Nine Practices (行柔弱):

> Practice flexibility and weakness.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> The excellence of water is that it is able to remain supple and weak. In this it images the Dao. Moving from high places to low, it avoids the solid and returns to emptiness, constantly benefiting the ten thousand things with its moisture. To the end, it does not struggle; this is why [the Dao] wishes people to take water as their model. … If people … were only able to imitate water in lacking contention, they would never meet with great harm.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 8, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), pp. 86–87

> 道氣微弱，故久在無所不伏。水法道柔弱，故能消穿崖石，道人當法之。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 36 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'The pneuma of the Dao is subtle and weak; so it endures and there is nothing it does not subdue. Water models itself on the Dao's suppleness and weakness, and so it can wear through cliffs and rock. People of the Dao should model themselves on this.' (on Laozi 36, 'The supple and weak overcome the hard and strong')

> 道甚廣大，處柔弱，不與俗人爭

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 34 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'The Dao is very vast, yet it dwells in suppleness and weakness and does not contend with common people.'

This is water's disposition: taking the low place, giving way rather than struggling, and still benefiting others. The commentary also reads it bodily, as making oneself supple like an infant by gathering pneuma. In conduct it shows as yielding instead of forcing one's way; it is not passivity toward others' needs.

The practice is partly physiological (pneuma and bodily suppleness), which single acts rarely reveal. 'Weakness' is not cowardice or refusing help: water 'constantly benefits'. ## Practice good deeds

Doing and accumulating good: kindness, trustworthy speech, and instructing others in goodness. In this text good deeds feed the body's essence and earn heaven's reward.

The sixth of the Nine Practices (行諸善):

> Practice good deeds.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> People should model themselves on water, constantly delighting in goodness and kindness. … People should constantly instruct one another in goodness and should be trustworthy.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 8, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), pp. 86–87

> The essences might be compared to the waters of a pond and the body to the embankments along the sides of the pond. Good deeds are like the water's source. … If one does not accumulate good deeds, the pond is cut off at its source and the water will dry up.

Bokenkamp, introduction to the Xiang'er (1997), p. 51

Bokenkamp's summary of the commentary's teaching on reward:

> the people will even perform good in secret, out of the fear that if their rulers and masters were to reward them, they would be deprived of Heaven's reward

Bokenkamp, introduction to the Xiang'er (1997), pp. 54–55

> 常為善，見惡人不棄也。就往教之，示道誡。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 27 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'He constantly does good; seeing evil people, he does not abandon them, but goes to teach them and shows them the precepts of the Dao.'

Good deeds here include everyday kindness and trustworthiness and the religious good of teaching others the precepts. They are to be accumulated and done sincerely; done for human reward they forfeit heaven's. This practice is the positive counterpart of the precept "Do not commit any of the various evil acts."

The motive structure (longevity, heavenly tallies) is part of the teaching. The commentary's attack on Confucian morality as insincere is its own polemic. Ordinary work is not itself a good deed. ## Practice knowing how to cease with sufficiency

Knowing when one has enough and stopping there: not pressing on for higher rank or more wealth, and turning surplus to giving.

The eighth of the Nine Practices (行知止足):

> Practice knowing how to cease with sufficiency.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> 王侯承先人之後。既有名，當知止足，不得復思高尊強求也。… 諸知止足，終不危殆。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 32 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Kings and lords inherit from their forebears. Once they have a name, they should know how to cease with sufficiency, and not think of strenuously seeking higher honour. … All who know how to cease with sufficiency will never be in peril.' (on Laozi 32)

> 故誡知止足，令人於世間裁自如，便思施惠散財除殃，不敢多求。奉道誡者，可長處吉不兇，不能止足，相返不虛也。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 36 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Hence the precept to know how to cease with sufficiency: it makes people in the world keep themselves in measure [裁自如, uncertain], think of bestowing kindness and dispersing wealth to dispel calamity, and not dare to seek much. Those who keep the precepts of the Dao can dwell long in good fortune, not ill; for those who cannot cease with sufficiency, the reversal is no empty saying.' (on Laozi 36)

> 富貴貧賤，各自守道為務，至誠者道與之，貧賤者無自鄙強欲求富貴也。不強求者，為不失其所故久也。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 33 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Rich or poor, high or low, each should make keeping to the Dao their task; the Dao gives to the utterly sincere. The poor and lowly should not despise themselves and strenuously seek wealth and rank. Those who do not seek strenuously do not lose their place, and so endure.' (on Laozi 33)

> 陳力殖穀，裁令自足。… 多望不止則或，或耶歸之也。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 22 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Put your strength into growing grain and keep it to what suffices you. … If one's hopes are many and never stop, one is led astray, and being astray one returns to deviance.' (on Laozi 22)

> 17. Do not pamper your body with good clothes and fine foods. … 19. Do not, through poverty, seek strenuously after wealth.

Xiang'er precepts, trans. Bokenkamp (1997), pp. 49–50

This is the one Nine Practices item the commentary names in its own words (知止足). Knowing sufficiency means accepting the name, rank or means one has without strenuous seeking for more, keeping one's grain to what suffices, and turning surplus into gifts. It binds rich and poor alike: the poor should not despise themselves and grasp for wealth.

The retrieved Bokenkamp excerpt ends at the commentary on Laozi 16, so these passages on Laozi 22–36 are quoted in Chinese with project translation; 裁自如 is uncertain. The text sets no measure of sufficiency; meeting real need is not grasping.

## Practice lacking falseness

Wu wei read as 'lacking artifice': not acting from wilful, contrived passions, which this text equates with not doing evil. It is not idleness.

The first of the Nine Practices (行無為):

> Practice lacking falseness.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> Adherents are not enjoined not to act, but to still their hearts, to quiet the winds of passion, and to make the movements of the Dao their own through following its precepts. They are forbidden to act in evil ways, which for this text means "contrived" ways that originate in the human will and passions.

Bokenkamp, introduction to the Xiang'er (1997), pp. 51–52

> The newborn does not act willfully [wu wei] and is thus joined with the Dao. … Those of higher attainment have open minds and naturally … increase their knowledge. Knowing evil, they discard it; knowing good, they are able to practice it. They dare not perform evil

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 10, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), pp. 90–91

Bokenkamp's footnote on the line 'let there be no false action [wu wei]':

> Here wu wei is glossed as "not performing evil deeds."

Bokenkamp, footnote to his translation of the Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 10, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 91

> 道性不為惡事，故能神無所不作，道人當法之。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 37 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'It is the nature of the Dao not to do evil deeds; therefore its spirit can do everything. People of the Dao should model themselves on this.' (on Laozi 37, 'The Dao constantly does nothing, yet nothing is left undone')

So 'lacking falseness' is not a call to inaction. It forbids acting from contrived will and passion, which the commentary equates with doing evil, and commends acting from a stilled heart in line with the precepts. In conduct it shows as refraining from scheming or wilful wrong.

Bokenkamp notes that wu wei is usually translated 'inaction' or 'nonintervention'; his 'lacking falseness' follows this text's reading of wei as 'contrived'. ## Practice lacking fame

Not seeking fame, glory or honour, hiding one's good deeds, and withdrawing once one's work is done.

The fourth of the Nine Practices (行無名):

> Practice lacking fame.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> Fame and accomplishment are the enemies of the body; when they are achieved, the body is obliterated. This is why the Dao prohibits them. … When Fan Li departed by boat, it was because he humbly trusted the intentions of the Dao. His body was not destroyed when he went into reclusion; this is the efficacy of the Dao.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 9, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 88

> The Dao does not delight in those who strenuously seek after status and honor. Where there is favor, disgrace always accompanies it. … If, on the other hand, one were to accord with the intentions of the Dao, one would possess a body but not … cherish it, would never seek glory and favor, would not be profligate in eating and drinking, would always be frugal, and would hide good actions.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 13, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), pp. 94–95

> 11. Do not covet glory or seek it strenuously. 12. Do not seek fame. … 26. Do not proclaim [yourself to be a] Sage or contribute to the fame of the mighty.

Xiang'er precepts, trans. Bokenkamp (1997), pp. 49–50

> 是故默而行之，不欲見功。今之臣子，雖忠孝，皆欲以買君父，求功名。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 18 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Therefore they act in silence and do not wish their merit to be seen. Ministers and sons today, though loyal and filial, all want to use it to buy favour from lord and father and to seek merit and fame.'

The commentary treats fame as dangerous to the body and offensive to the Dao. Lacking fame shows as declining credit and honour, doing good in secret, refusing to be hailed as a sage, and withdrawing after success as Fan Li did. Good conduct that becomes known without one's seeking is not a fault.

The stated rationale is bodily longevity and heavenly reward, not modesty for its own sake. Accepting an office one is given is not ruled out; the commentary objects to strenuous seeking. ## Practice maintaining the feminine. Do not initiate actions.

Patterning oneself on the stable, receptive 'feminine' (earth). In this text it chiefly means a man conserving his essence by restraint in sexual activity, and not pushing himself first.

The third of the Nine Practices (行守雌，勿先動):

> Practice maintaining the feminine. Do not initiate actions.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> The injunction that one should "maintain the feminine" and not "initiate actions," as well as numbers 2 and 18 of the Xiang'er precepts, are most likely related to the practice of sexual abstinence outside of reproduction and the ritual "merging the pneumas."

Bokenkamp, introduction to the Xiang'er (1997), p. 51

> The "feminine" is earth. The inborn nature of its body is stable. Women are patterned on it; therefore [their sexual organs] do not become rigid. If a man wishes to congeal his essence he should mentally pattern himself on earth and be like a woman. He should not work to give himself priority.

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 6, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 83

> At the age of fifty, having filled one's [productive] role, one should stop. … Even when one is young, though one possesses [the capabilities for reproduction], one should rest [from intercourse] and preserve [these potencies].

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 6, trans. Stephen R. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), pp. 84–85

> 欲令雄如雌。… 行《玄女經》、龔子、容成之法，悉欲貸，何人主當貸若者乎？故令不得也。唯有自守，絕心閉念者，大無極也。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 28 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: '[On 'know the male, keep to the female':] It means making the male like the female. … Those who practise the methods of the Dark Maiden Scripture, Gongzi and Rongcheng all want to borrow [essence]; who would lend to them? So it is not permitted. Only one who keeps to himself, cutting off the heart's longings and closing off thoughts, reaches the great limitless.'

Here 'feminine' is not a social role assigned to women. The commentary asks men to model themselves on the earth's stability: to conserve essence, to limit intercourse to reproduction (stopping at fifty and resting even when young), and to reject sexual techniques that 'borrow' a partner's essence. 'Do not initiate actions' fits the same frame: he 'should not work to give himself priority'.

Bokenkamp's link of 'do not initiate actions' to sexual practice is his scholarly judgement ('most likely'). The rule addresses men's bodies; applying it to women or to social initiative in general is not attested, and starting a needed task is not shown to violate it. ## Practice yielding to others

Giving way to others: taking the lesser share, stepping aside, bearing anger rather than giving it, and not contending over right and wrong.

The ninth of the Nine Practices (行推讓):

> Practice yielding to others.

Nine Practices, trans. Bokenkamp, Early Daoist Scriptures (1997), p. 49

> 14. Place yourself in a humble position. … 25. Do not contend with others over right and wrong. When you meet the contentious, flee them.

Xiang'er precepts, trans. Bokenkamp (1997), pp. 49–50

> 又一說曰，道人寧施人勿為人所施，寧避人勿為人所避，寧教人為善勿為人所教，寧為人所怒勿怒人，分均寧與人多勿為人所與多。

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 36 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Another explanation says: a person of the Dao would rather give to others than be given to; rather make way for others than have others make way; rather teach others to do good than be taught; rather be the object of others' anger than be angry at others; and in dividing shares would rather give others more than be given more.' (on Laozi 36)

> 我是若非，勿與之爭也。… 聖人不與俗人爭，有爭避之高逝

Xiang'er commentary on Laozi 22 (Dunhuang S.6825; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Whether I am right or wrong, do not contend with them. … The sage does not contend with common people; when there is contention he avoids it and goes far away.' (on Laozi 22)

Yielding is concrete here: giving others the larger share, stepping aside rather than making others step aside, accepting others' anger instead of venting one's own, and leaving a quarrel over who is right instead of pressing it.

The Nine Practices list gives only the heading; its meaning comes from associated precepts and from an explanation the commentary itself marks as 'another explanation'. The sources speak of one's own share and one's own quarrels, not of surrendering others' rightful claims entrusted to one.
