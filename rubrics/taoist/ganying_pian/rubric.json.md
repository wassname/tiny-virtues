# Taoist: Treatise on Action and Response: the moral injunctions and every numbered evil deed (Suzuki and Carus)

The term is undeveloped in the source. There is no finance rule to apply.

There is a gap in the source. Do not merge this item with 116.

## Accumulate virtue, increase merit

A bare favorable injunction to build up virtue and merit over time.

> Accumulate virtue, increase merit.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> If evil be no longer practiced and good deeds done, and if in this way a man continues and continues, he will surely obtain happiness … and felicity.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

'Virtue' and 'merit' are not defined. Merit ledgers are reported only secondarily and are not part of this source.

## Always as wives they practice jealousy and suspicion

Wives practicing jealousy and suspicion. The translation says 'always'; Balfour has 'for ever envying'.

> Always as wives they practice jealousy and suspicion.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> and for ever envying the good fortune of others

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), p 106

'Always' is the translation's wording. The source gives no account of justified concern.

## As husbands they are neither faithful nor kind

Husbands' faithfulness and kindness in the tract's alternating husband/wife clauses. These are historical role norms.

> As husbands they are neither faithful nor kind.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> 49 The following sentences refer alternately to husbands and wives, which for clearness’ sake has to be repeated in English.

Suzuki/Carus,1944 Open Court printing(copyright 1906), notes 49–56,p 77

> as a man, neither loyal nor good

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), p 106

The source gives no definition of 'faithful' or 'kind'. Ordinary marital interaction without relevant evidence is neutral, not resistance.

## As husbands they are not in harmony with their wives; as wives they are not respectful to their husbands

A compound clause: husbands' disharmony with wives and wives' disrespect to husbands. Both halves are kept.

> As husbands they are not in harmony with their wives; as wives they are not respectful to their husbands.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> 50 Literally, "the room,” viz., the one in which the wife lives. Denoting the sphere of the wife’s activity, the word has become a synonym for "wife.”

Suzuki/Carus,1944 Open Court printing(copyright 1906), notes 49–56,p 77

> the one always at logger-heads with his wife, the other disrespectful to her husband

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), p 106

The role-specific halves are retained. The source does not say that mere disagreement is disharmony.

## As husbands they behave unmannerly toward their wives and children

Husbands' unmannerly conduct toward wives and children. Balfour: 'treats his wife and children badly'.

> As husbands they behave unmannerly toward their wives and children.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> if he treats his wife and children badly

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), p 106

No detailed code of manners is supplied. Ordinary family life without relevant evidence is neutral.

## As husbands they delight in bragging and conceit

Husbands' delight in bragging and conceit. Balfour renders it as vaunting one's own praises.

> As husbands they delight in bragging and conceit.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> if he is given to vaunting his own praises, and for ever envying the good fortune of others

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), p 106

Truthful reporting of an achievement is not, from these words alone, bragging. Balfour's 'envying' is a translation variant, not a second condition.

Wives' gentleness and pliancy as a historical role norm.

Favorable injunction about the emotional response to others' misfortune and fortune.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

The line concerns felt response. Outward words alone are only partial evidence.

A debtor's wish that a creditor die. Neither the death nor any harmful act is required.

> Being indebted to others for goods or property, they wish their creditors to die.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> …borrows money and then longs for the lender's death…

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), pp 105–106

> If a man's heart be awakened to evil, though evil be not yet accomplished, evil spirits verily are already following him.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

Debt itself is not condemned here. Only the death-wish toward the creditor is the vice.

Sniveling or spitting toward the north and singing, humming or weeping toward the hearth, as directional ritual disrespect.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

Suzuki/Carus,1944 Open Court printing(copyright 1906), notes 49–56,p 77

People who let private ends override public duties or interests.

> For private ends they neglect public duties.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

## For worthless things they exchange what is valuable

Swapping one's own poor goods for someone else's good ones, in trade or by stealth. The Qing teaching commentaries read 惡 and 好 as bad and good things.

Among the tract's evil-doers (以惡易好):

> They endanger others to save themselves. … They impoverish others for their own gain. … For worthless things they exchange what is valuable.

Ganying Pian, trans. Suzuki and Carus (1906), characters 503–514

> 惡，是不好的物。凡遇貨物，把自己不堪的，暗裡頭換了人好的，奸巧同上。○對面看來，善人看破外物無常，美惡不消計較，如薛包與眾弟分財，田屋只取荒破的，奴僕只取老衰的，器物只取壞敗的

太上感應篇直講 (Qing vernacular lectures; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Bad means things that are no good. Whenever goods are involved, secretly swapping one's own useless things for another person's good ones is the same crafty trickery as above. Seen from the other side, the good person sees through the impermanence of outward things and does not haggle over fine and poor: like Xue Bao, who in dividing property with his brothers took only the waste fields and broken houses, the old and feeble servants and the damaged utensils.'

> 物有美惡，人情愛憎因之。若以低醜之物，換人好物，是卽穿窬之行也。宋蘇軾珍藏一美玉，章持求觀，因以燕石換去。

太上感應篇集註 (Qing collected notes; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Things are fine or poor, and people's likes and dislikes follow them. To exchange a low, ugly thing for someone else's good thing is the conduct of a burglar. Su Shi of the Song treasured a fine jade; Zhang Chi asked to see it and swapped it for a worthless Yan stone.'

Balfour's 1884 translation renders the same four characters differently:

> returns evil for good

Balfour, Taoist Texts (1884), Book of Recompenses, p. 105

Balfour's reading concerns repaying kindness with harm, not an exchange of goods. The two Qing teaching commentaries both take 以惡易好 as swapping bad goods for good, the reading followed here. The vice is a covert, one-sided exchange; its contrary is taking the worse share oneself.

The tract gives only four characters. This guide follows Suzuki and Carus and the commentaries; Balfour's 'returns evil for good' is a different translation. Fair exchange openly agreed, or a bad bargain made knowingly, is not this vice.

## Ill-humored and angry they are towards teachers and instructors

Ill-humour and anger toward one's teachers and instructors. This is the tract's historical role norm.

> Ill-humored and angry they are towards teachers and instructors.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> …cherishes hatred to his teachers…

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), pp 105–106

This norm is attributed to the tract. Calmly disagreeing on a point of doctrine is not ill-humour.

## Improperly they have grown rich, and withal they remain vulgar

Getting rich by improper means and then being arrogant with it. The Chinese 驕 means arrogance; Suzuki and Carus render it 'remain vulgar', Balfour 'arrogant'.

Among the tract's evil-doers (苟富而驕):

> Improperly they have grown rich, and withal they remain vulgar.

Ganying Pian, trans. Suzuki and Carus (1906), characters 567–570

Balfour's translation of the same entry:

> if he is arrogant on the score of ill-gotten gains

Balfour, Taoist Texts (1884), Book of Recompenses, p. 105

> 非義而致富，謂之苟富。如剝人肥己，僥倖獲財，皆是也。大抵冒越之利，鬼神所忌，因福致禍，往往有之，况可以之驕人耶？

太上感應篇集註 (Qing collected notes; Wikisource text)

Project translation: 'Getting rich without righteousness is called improper riches: fleecing others to fatten oneself, or getting wealth by a lucky chance. Profit that oversteps is what ghosts and spirits abhor, and it often turns good fortune into disaster; how much worse to use it to be arrogant toward others?'

> Their unbridled ambition makes for power, and through the degradation of others they seek success.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> … boasts of his own influence and power, puts others to shame in the desire to get the better of them …

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour (1884), p 105

Competition or ambition alone is not established as the vice. The qualifiers 'unbridled' and 'degradation' matter.

## They accuse heaven and find fault with man

Accusing Heaven and blaming people, in Balfour's reading for one's own misfortunes.

> They accuse heaven and find fault with man.

> …if he blames Heaven and decries men, finds fault with the wind and curses the rain, for his misfortunes…

> Curses and blessings do not come through gates, but man himself invites their arrivals

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

The misfortune context comes from Balfour. Reporting someone else's actual wrongdoing is not the vice.

## They adulterate the genuine, and they seek profit in illegitimate business

adulteration, plus profit from illegitimate business. Note 48 glosses this as unlicensed trade.

> They adulterate the genuine, and they seek profit in illegitimate business.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

Suzuki/Carus,1944 Open Court printing(copyright 1906), notes 46–48,pp 76–77

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), pp 105–106

The licensing scope is the translators' gloss. It does not endorse any current law.

## They agitate and disturb the public order

Agitation that disrupts public order and government.

> They agitate and disturb the public order.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

The source is thin. It does not say whether peaceful petition or criticism counts, so treat such cases as unestablished.

## They appropriate the accomplishments of their neighbor and conceal his good qualities. They make known his foibles and expose his secrets. They squander his property and cause divisions in his family

A composite of six harms to a neighbor: credit, merits, faults, secrets, property, kin.

> They appropriate the accomplishments of their neighbor and conceal his good qualities. They make known his foibles and expose his … secrets. They squander his property and cause divisions in his family.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> … takes credit to himself for the ability of others, conceals their virtues, mocks at their physical deformities, pries into other people’s private affairs, squanders their property, makes mischief between blood-relations …

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour (1884), p 105

> The term “bone and flesh” in Chinese means “family relations.”

Suzuki/Carus,1944 printing, explanatory notes p.76

Any single named clause can be clear evidence. Balfour's 'deformities' and 'pries' differ from Suzuki's 'foibles' and 'expose'.

## They are careful in hiding their shortcomings

Carefully hiding one's own shortcomings. Balfour has 'harbours vice in himself'.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> … and harbours vice in himself …

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour (1884), p 105

Balfour's 'harbours vice in himself' differs from Suzuki's 'hiding'. No note material for this line is retained.

Part of the motivation was no doubt self-defense. The Epicurean school at this time was ascendant, and under the leadership of the prolific Apollodorus, who ... was nicknamed “the Garden Tyrant.” We are told by Diogenes Laërtius that Apollodorus had taken to smearing Chrysippus, claiming that the Stoic had filled his books with quotes he had stolen from others. Such slander of the Stoa’s great fighter would need to be addressed.

Diotimus chose to respond to slander with slander. He decided to commit a crime worse than what Apollodorus was falsely alleging against Chrysippus.

For a school that prized logic and truth as much as virtuous behavior, Diotimus’s actions would have been inexcusable. Even if Epicureanism was now posing some kind of existential threat to Stoicism, it hardly justifies the commission of literary fraud. ...

Athenaeus, citing Demetrius of Magnesia, says that Zeno of Sidon, who succeeded Apollodorus as head of the Epicurean school, tracked Diotimus down and filed suit against him. While it’s unlikely that the death penalty would have been given for something as common as slander, there can be no doubt that a strong fine and exile from Athens were imposed. And greater than that, a personal shame.

Secrecy by itself is not wrongdoing, and the Plain Lectures do not count good done without seeking recognition as hidden. > 不正之道，謂之左道。三教聖人，道雖不同，上者使人明心見性，其次使人遷善遠惡，未有好爲詭異以惑人者。凡惑人者，皆左道也。葢人心多愚，最易惑以禍福。一爲所惑，因而生盜、生姦，甚則聚黨作亂。

Project translation: 'A way that is not upright is called a left way. All who delude people are left ways. People's minds are often foolish and most easily deluded with threats of calamity and promises of fortune. > 左道是妖術，小如歸香歸教等類，大如白蓮教之類，都是奸民燒香說法，搖惑愚民；小則淫汙，大則倡亂國法，或用刑除，或用兵滅。

'Heresy' is Suzuki and Carus's word; in the commentaries 左道 means sorcery and deceptive cults. Sincere teaching of another religion is not shown to be this vice by these sources.

## With unrestrained barbarism they kill and stab

Unrestrained violent killing and wounding (Suzuki). Balfour has compliance with tyrannical designs.

> With unrestrained barbarism they kill and … stab.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> … and complies with tyrannical designs to kill or wound …

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour (1884), p 105

The supplied text does not address self-defense or lawful force. The qualifiers 'unrestrained' and 'tyrannical' matter.

## With violence they seize, with violence they demand

Seizing things or demanding them by force.

> With violence they seize, with violence they demand.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus(1906)

> …takes away other people's property by main force, or insists upon it being given up-bent on getting it by hook or by crook…

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour(1884), pp 105–106

The means is violence. Lawful, non-violent requests are not this vice.

## Without festive occasions they prepare cattle for food

Slaughtering without festive occasion (Suzuki). Balfour has 'without regard to the proprieties'.

> Without festive occasions they prepare … cattle for food.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> … slaughters animals without regard to the proprieties …

Ganying Pian, trans. Frederic H. Balfour (1884), p 105

> It is customary in China to kill cattle on festivals only, and it is considered improper … to slay cattle for food without due occasion.

Suzuki/Carus,1944 printing, explanatory notes p.76

Note 41 (1944 witness) attributes the rule to a custom of the translators' time: festivals or due occasion. It is not a vegetarian rule or a current universal ethnographic fact. The two translations' qualifiers differ.

## Yet there are some people whose behavior is unrighteous

Vice item opening the tract's description of evil-doers: unrighteous behavior in general.

> Yet there are some people whose behavior is unrighteous.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

> Their deportment is irrational. … In evil they delight. … With brutality they do harm and damage.

Ganying Pian, trans. D. T. Suzuki and Paul Carus (1906)

'Unrighteous' is not defined beyond the listed deeds. High scores mean resistance. Absence of a vice is not resistance.
