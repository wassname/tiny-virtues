# Taoist: Laozi's three greatest faults (ch. 46)

## The wish to be getting

Laozi 46's third fault, against contentment; ch. 44 links large stores with loss.

> … no fault greater than the wish to be getting. Therefore the sufficiency of contentment is an enduring and unchanging sufficiency.

Laozi, Daodejing 46, trans. James Legge(1891)

> Who loves large stores Gives up the richer state. … Who knows to stop Incurs no blame.

Laozi, Daodejing 44, trans. James Legge(1891)

This does not condemn earning or acquiring as such, and the text sets no threshold for 'sufficient'.

## To be discontented with one's lot

Laozi 46's second fault, against the 'sufficiency of contentment'; ch. 44 adds knowing when to stop.

> 2. There is no guilt greater than to sanction ambition; no calamity greater than to be discontented with one's lot; … Therefore the sufficiency of contentment is an enduring and unchanging sufficiency.

Laozi, Daodejing 46, trans. James Legge(1891)

> Who is content Needs fear no shame. Who knows to stop Incurs no blame.

Laozi, Daodejing 44, trans. James Legge(1891)

The text does not define what one's lot is, and the case of real deprivation is unresolved.

## To sanction ambition

Laozi 46's first craving fault in Legge's rendering, set beside the war-horse image and contentment.

> 46. 1. When the Tao prevails in the world, they send back their swift horses to (draw) the dung-carts. When the Tao is disregarded in the world, the war-horses breed in the border lands. 2. There is no guilt greater than to sanction ambition; … Therefore the sufficiency of contentment is an enduring and unchanging sufficiency.

Laozi, Daodejing 46, trans. James Legge(1891)

> Who cleaves to fame Rejects what is more great; …

Laozi, Daodejing 44, trans. James Legge(1891)

Legge renders this 'sanction ambition'.

This reading depends on Legge's rendering. It does not condemn every goal or the act of earning a living.
