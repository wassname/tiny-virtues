# Islamic: Al-Ghazali’s four fundamental good traits of character (Ihya, book 22)

## Courage

Ghazali: Courage as the irascible faculty (anger) subjected to intellect, not merely battlefield bravery.

> **By ‘Courage’ we refer to the subjection of the irascible faculty to the intellect** ...

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995).

> **As for the trait of Courage, this gives rise to nobility [karam], intrepidity [najda]**, manliness [shahama], greatness of soul, ... endurance [Ultima!], clemency [hilm], steadfastness [thabat], the suppression of rage [kazm al-ghayz], dignity [waqar], affection ... and other such praiseworthy qualities.

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995).

> The irascible faculty, when sound and balanced, is called ‘Courage’ ... Should the former faculty lose its balance and incline towards excess it is called ‘recklessness’, while should it incline towards weakness and insufficiency it is termed ‘cowardice’ and ‘languor’. ... **The mean is the praiseworthy thing** ...

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995).

> striving with one’s self is Courage, which proceeds from the use of the irascible faculty under the control of the intellect and with just moderation.

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995).

## Justice

Ghazali: Justice as inner potency regulating anger and desire as Wisdom directs; not merely social fairness.

> …by Justice a condition and potency in the soul by which it controls the expansion and contraction of anger and desire as directed by Wisdom.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> As for the faculty to effect a just equilibrium, it is this which sets desire and anger under the command of the intellect and the Law.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> …indicating that severity and compassion both have their place: perfection is not to be found through severity or compassion in every situation.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> The faculty for just equilibrium, however, when in disorder, has no extremes of excess and defect; rather it has one opposite, which is tyranny.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Perfect equilibrium is attributed to the Prophet alone; others differ by degree. That is not an eligibility rule for conduct evidence.

## Temperance

Ghazali: Temperance as appetite disciplined by intellect and Law; balanced, not eliminated.

> ...while **by ‘Temperance’ we have in mind the disciplining of the appetitive faculty by the intellect and the Law.**

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995)

> ...the appetitive faculty, when sound and balanced, is known as ‘Temperance’... Should the appetitive faculty move to the point of excess it is called ‘cupidity’, while if it should incline to defect it is known as ‘indifference’. **The mean is the praiseworthy thing, and it is this which constitutes virtue, while the two extremes are blameworthy vices.**

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995)

> As for the quality of Temperance, this gives rise to generosity ... **contentedness with one’s lot** ... helping others ... and **absence of craving** ...

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, trans. Winter (1995)

Temperance disciplines appetite 'by the intellect and the Law'. Refusing what the Law forbids, or a chosen restraint that still meets real needs, is not the defect; 'indifference' is appetite failing even for what is lawful and needed.

## Wisdom

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII (On Disciplining the Soul), Winter's 1995 translation, pp. 19-21: wisdom as the soundness of the rational faculty and its mean.

> Therefore the fundamental good traits of character are four in number: Wisdom, Courage, Temperance and Justice. By ‘Wisdom’ we mean a condition of the soul by which it distinguishes true from false in all volitional acts, by Justice a condition and potency in the soul by which it controls the expansion and contraction of anger and desire as directed by Wisdom. By ‘Courage’ we refer to the subjection of the irascible faculty to the intellect, while by ‘Temperance’ we have in mind the disciplining of the appetitive faculty by the intellect and the Law.

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, tr. Winter, pp. 20-21

> The faculty for just equilibrium, however, when in disorder, has no extremes of excess and defect; rather it has one opposite, which is tyranny. As for Wisdom, exceeding the bounds in its regard by using it for corrupt ends is called ‘swindling’ and ‘fraud’, while its insufficient application is termed ‘stupidity’. Again, it is the mean to which the word ‘Wisdom’ is applied.

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, tr. Winter, p. 20

> The rational faculty is sound and good when it is easily able to discriminate, that is, to distinguish honesty from lies in speech, truth from falsehood in questions of belief, and beauty from ugliness in actions. When this faculty is sound it bears fruit in the form of Wisdom, which is the chief of the good traits of character

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, tr. Winter, p. 19

> when the intellect is balanced it will bring forth discretion ... and excellence of discernment ... penetration of thought ... and correctness of conjecture ... and an understanding of the subtle implications of actions and the hidden defects of the soul. When unbalanced in the direction of excess, then cunning, ... swindling, ... deception and slyness result, and when in that of defect, then stupidity ..., inexperience ..., foolishness ..., heedlessness ... and insanity are the consequences.

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, tr. Winter, p. 21

> The difference between stupidity and insanity is that the intention of the stupid man is sound, only his means of realising it are defective

Al-Ghazali, Ihya XXII, tr. Winter, p. 21

Wisdom is discernment applied in choice: true from false in belief, honesty from lies in speech, fair from foul in action. Clever intellect used for corrupt ends is its excess (cunning, swindling, deception); failing to apply it is its defect (stupidity, heedlessness). It can overlap with Justice, which governs anger and desire as Wisdom directs.

## cowardice

Ghazali: cowardice as the irascible faculty's weakness and insufficiency, paired with languor.

> …while should it incline towards weakness and insufficiency it is termed ‘cowardice’ and ‘languor’. … The mean is the praiseworthy thing, and it is this which constitutes virtue, while the two extremes are blameworthy vices.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> …and when on the side of defect, to ignominy [mahana], self-abasement [dhilla], cowardice [jaza c ], meanness … [khasasa], lack of resolution [fighar al-nafs ], and holding oneself back from doing that which is right and obligatory.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Overlaps languor; the source gives no separate meaning. Avoiding an unnecessary fight is not cowardice.

## cupidity

Ghazali: cupidity as excess of the appetitive faculty beyond Temperance's mean.

> Should the appetitive faculty move to the point of excess it is called ‘cupidity’… The mean is the praiseworthy thing, and it is this which constitutes virtue, while the two extremes are blameworthy vices.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> When it deviates towards excess or defect, greed [hir$], cupidity [sharah] and obscenity [waqaha] result, as do spite… extravagance [tabdhir]… ostentation… flattery [malq], envy [hasad]… self-abasement before the rich, disdain for the poor, and so forth.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Branch list mixes excess and defect results without assignment; do not fix each to cupidity.

## fraud

Ghazali: fraud, named with swindling as Wisdom's excess for corrupt ends.

> As for Wisdom, exceeding the bounds in its regard by using it for corrupt ends is called ‘swindling’ and ‘fraud’, while its insufficient application is termed ‘stupidity’.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> When unbalanced in the direction of excess, then cunning, … swindling, … deception and slyness result…

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

No meaning distinct from swindling is supplied.

Footnote markers on these words are Winter's; do not treat translator notes as Ghazali.

## indifference

Ghazali (tr. Winter): 'indifference' as the appetitive faculty's defect below Temperance's mean, explicitly applied to the desire for food.

> Should the appetitive faculty move to the point of excess it is called ‘cupidity’, while if it should incline to defect it is known as ‘indifference’. The mean is the praiseworthy thing, and it is this which constitutes virtue, while the two extremes are blameworthy vices.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

The view in question is that spiritual discipline completely suppresses desire.

> Such a view is absurd, for desire has been created for a purpose, and is an indispensable part of human nature: should the desire for food cease man would die… What is required is not the total extirpation of these things, but rather … the restoration of their balance and moderation, which is the middle point between excess and defect.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.3, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 27–29

> Likewise is the case with the desire for food: [moderation] should prevail, rather than greed or indifference. God (Exalted is He!) has said, Eat and drink, but be not extravagant, for God loves not the extravagant.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.3, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 27–29

Source names food-indifference and rejects extirpating desire. It gives no quantity threshold and does not condemn fasts prescribed by Law. It does not address illness-related appetite loss. Little eating alone is not evidence of vice.

## languor

Ghazali: languor, named alongside cowardice for the irascible faculty's weakness and insufficiency.

> …while should it incline towards weakness and insufficiency it is termed ‘cowardice’ and ‘languor’. … The mean is the praiseworthy thing…

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> …when on the side of defect, to ignominy [mahana], self-abasement [dhilla]… lack of resolution [fighar al-nafs ], and holding oneself back from doing that which is right and obligatory.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

No distinct definition of languor is supplied; treat it with cowardice.

Do not read languor as physical laziness; the source attaches it only to irascible defect.

## recklessness

Ghazali: recklessness as the irascible faculty's excess beyond Courage's mean.

> The irascible faculty, when sound and balanced, is called ‘Courage’… Should the former faculty lose its balance and incline towards excess it is called ‘recklessness’… The mean is the praiseworthy thing, and it is this which constitutes virtue, while the two extremes are blameworthy vices.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> When unbalanced on the side of excess, which is recklessness, it leads to arrogance [$alaf], conceit [badhkh], quickness to anger [istishafa], pride [takabbur] and vainglory [ Q ujb]…

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Mere calmness or absence of anger is not resistance; deficiency is a separate vice (cowardice/languor).

## stupidity

Ghazali: stupidity as Wisdom's insufficient application: sound intention, defective means.

> …while its insufficient application is termed ‘stupidity’. Again, it is the mean to which the word ‘Wisdom’ is applied.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> By inexperience I mean an insufficient experience which is nonetheless combined with sound understanding: a man may be inexperienced in one matter and not in another. The difference between stupidity and insanity is that the intention of the stupid man is sound, only his means of realising it are defective, since he is not possessed of a correct understanding of how to follow the way leading to his goal; the madman, on the other hand, chooses that which should not be chosen…

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Inexperience and mere failure are not stupidity; wrong ends belong to insanity, not this vice.

## swindling

Ghazali: swindling as Wisdom's excess, using the rational faculty for corrupt ends; paired with fraud.

> As for Wisdom, exceeding the bounds in its regard by using it for corrupt ends is called ‘swindling’ and ‘fraud’, while its insufficient application is termed ‘stupidity’. Again, it is the mean to which the word ‘Wisdom’ is applied.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> When unbalanced in the direction of excess, then cunning, … swindling, … deception and slyness result…

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Identical to fraud in this source; no independent meaning. Intelligence alone is not the vice.

## tyranny

Ghazali: tyranny as the single opposite of the disordered faculty of just equilibrium.

> The faculty for just equilibrium, however, when in disorder, has no extremes of excess and defect; rather it has one opposite, which is tyranny.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

> …by Justice a condition and potency in the soul by which it controls the expansion and contraction of anger and desire as directed by Wisdom.

Ghazali, Ihya XXII.2, trans. T. J. Winter (ITS 1995), pp 18–23

Tyranny is read as failure of that control; the source gives no further description.

Source does not confine tyranny to rulers; political oppression examples are illustrations, not its definition.
