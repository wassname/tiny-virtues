# Islamic: Spending, anger-restraint and pardon among the righteous (Qur'an 3:134)

## Those who spend (freely), whether in prosperity, or in adversity

The first clause of 3:134, describing the righteous, per Ali note 453.

> …prepared for the righteous,- Those who spend (freely), whether in prosperity, or in adversity;…

Qur’an 3:134, trans. Abdullah Yusuf Ali

> 453 Another definition of the righteous (vv 134 35) So far from grasping material wealth, they give freely, of themselves and their substance not only when they are well off and it is easy for them to do so but also when they are in difficulties for other people may be in difficulties at the same time…

Abdullah Yusuf Ali, Holy Quran commentary (1934), note 453

The source sets no amounts. Overlaps charity and liberality.

## pardon (all) men

The third clause of 3:134, per Ali note 453; 42:40–41 adds context.

> …who restrain anger, and pardon (all) men;- for Allah loves those who do good;-

Qur’an 3:134, trans. Abdullah Yusuf Ali

> …And they do not throw the blame on others Even where such blame is due and correction is necessary their own mind is free from a sense of grievance for they forgive and cover other men's faults…

Abdullah Yusuf Ali, Holy Quran commentary (1934), note 453

> The recompense for an injury is an injury equal thereto (in degree): but if a person forgives and makes reconciliation, his reward is due from Allah… But indeed if any do help and defend themselves after a wrong (done) to them, against such there is no cause of blame.

Qur’an 42:40, 42:41, trans. Abdullah Yusuf Ali

Pardon allows necessary correction and self-defence. It means freedom from grievance and covering faults, not compulsory passivity.

'All' is the verse's scope. The source gives no rules for legal penalties.

## who restrain anger

The second clause of 3:134, per Ali note 453.

> ...003.134 Y: Those who spend (freely), whether in prosperity, or in adversity; **who restrain anger**, and pardon (all) men;- for Allah loves those who do good;-

Qur'an 3:133-135, trans. Yusuf Ali

> ...**They do not get ruffled in adversity or get angry when other people behave badly, or their own good plans fail On the contrary they redouble their efforts** For the charity—or good deed—is all the more necessary in adversity...

Yusuf Ali, commentary note 453 (1934), not Qur'an wording

<summary>old man - Some evidence that the conduct conflicts with this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
Once, in a fit of anger, he drove him from his cell.

Full passage:
A  certain  old  man  had  a  faithful  disciple. Once,  in  a  fit  of  anger,  he  drove  him  from  his  cell. The  disciple  waited  all  night  outside  the  door. In  the  morning  the  old  man  opened  it,  and,  when he  saw  him,  was  struck  with  shame,  and  said, "  You are  my  father  now,  because  your  humility  and patience  have  conquered  my  sin.  Come  in  again, and  from  henceforth  be  you  the  elder  and  the father.  I  will  be  the  disciple,  for  you  have  surpassed me,  though  I  am  aged."

Acted in a fit of anger, expelling a faithful disciple: failure to restrain anger. Later shame is separate (3:135).

<summary>Lycurgus the Lacedaemonian - Clear evidence that the conduct shows this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
this he refrained from doing, but brought him up and made a good man of him, and presented him in the theatre

Full passage:
What man among us does not admire the saying of Lycurgus the Lacedaemonian? For when he had been blinded in one eye by one of his fellow-citizens, and the people had turned over the young man to him, to take whatever vengeance upon the culprit he might desire, this he refrained from doing, but brought him up and made a good man of him, and presented him in the theatre. And when the Lacedaemonians expressed their surprise, he said, “This man when I received him at your hands was insolent and violent; I am returning him to you a reasonable and public-spirited person,”

Blinded by a citizen, offered vengeance, he refrained and reformed the man; restraining anger and pardoning, with redoubled good effort.

<summary>Varana - Neither direction is established by the described conduct.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
he saw, on the way, a family quarrel, through which some were slain. Distressed, he hastened to the Exalted One, and told him.

Full passage:
Reborn in this Buddha-age in Rosala as a brahmin's son, he [Varana] was named Varana. Come of age, he heard a Thera preach the Norm in a forest, and believing, entered the Order. One day going to wait upon the Buddha he saw, on the way, a family quarrel, through which some were slain. Distressed, he hastened to the Exalted One, and told him. And the latter, discerning the progress of his mind, exhorted him, saying:

Whoso here causeth fellow-creatures pain,
From this and from the other-world, from both
This man may forfeit all they yield of good.
Whoso with loving heart compassion takes
On every fellow-creature, such a man
Doth generate of merit ample store.

...

Train ye yourselves in pious utterance,
In waiting ever on the wise and good,
In haunting secret solitary seat,
And in the calm and concentrated mind.

When these verses were ended, Varana, developing insight, won arahantship.

Varana is distressed, not angered, by others' violence and seeks guidance; no anger shown, mild fit with not being ruffled.

<summary>Yen Hwúi - Some evidence that the conduct shows this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
a man whose angry feelings towards any particular person he did not suffer to visit upon another

Full passage:
When the Duke Ngai inquired which of the disciples were devoted to learning, Confucius answered him, "There was one Yen Hwúi who loved it--a man whose angry feelings towards any particular person he did not suffer to visit upon another; a man who would never fall into the same error twice. Unfortunately his allotted time was short, and he died, and now his like is not to be found; I have never heard of one so devoted to learning."

Yen Hwui did have angry feelings but restrained them from spreading to others. Partial restraint; anger toward the original person not shown restrained.

<summary>the Wolf - Some evidence that the conduct conflicts with this virtue.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
seized him and ate him up

Full passage:
WOLF, meeting with a Lamb astray from the fold, resolved not to lay violent hands on him, but to find some plea to justify to the Lamb the Wolf’s right to eat him. He thus addressed him: “Sirrah, last year you grossly insulted me.” “Indeed,” bleated the Lamb in a mournful tone of voice, “I was not then born.” Then said the Wolf, “You feed in my pasture.” “No, good sir,” replied the Lamb, “I have not yet tasted grass.” Again said the Wolf, “You drink of my well.” “No,” exclaimed the Lamb, “I never yet drank water, for as yet my mother’s milk is both food and drink to me.” Upon which the Wolf seized him and ate him up, saying, “Well! I won’t remain supperless, even though you refute every one of my imputations.” The tyrant will always find a pretext for his tyranny.

Wolf invents grievances ('you grossly insulted me') as pretext, then kills. Motive is hunger, not anger per se; false grievance and violence oppose restraining anger and pardoning.

Full passage:
Years back, when the earl of Ch'ing [Woo-shang, duke Chwang, the earl] had wished to make Kaou K'eu-me one of his high ministers, duke Ch'aou [then the earl’s son Hwuh], who disliked Kaou, had remonstrated strongly against such a measure. The earl did not listen to him; but when duke Ch'aou succeeded to the State, Kaou was afraid lest he should put him to death. On the day Sin-maou, therefore, he took the initiative, and killed duke Ch'aou, raising up his brother We in his room. A superior man will say that the prince knew the man whom he disliked. Kung-tsze Tah said, “Kaou Pih [Kaou K'eu-me] indeed deserved an evil end! His revenge of an ill done to him was excessive.”

Kaou kills the duke from fear of punishment over old dislike; harbors grievance, does not pardon. Fear more than anger stated.
