# Confucian: Seven grounds for divorce (七出 qi chu), the five that are acts

## Immorality

Typical Women of China, preface: 'immorality', third of the seven causes. In the sources it is the sexual ground, which the Lienü zhuan calls lasciviousness, not wrongdoing in general.

The compiler's preface (the scan reads 'nndutifulness'):

> There are seven causes for which a wife may be divorced, viz., nndutifulness towards her husband's parents; having no son; immorality; jealousy ; having a leprous disease ; talkativeness ; stealing.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

In the Lienü zhuan a wife recites a related list:

> According to the Seven Reasons for Divorce, jealousy is most properly the chief reason, while lasciviousness, stealing, loquaciousness, arrogance, childlessness, and suffering from a repulsive disease are all secondary.

Lienü zhuan 2.7, tr. Kinney (2014)

A modern historian summarizing the ritual commentaries:

> Licentiousness as a ground for divorce reflects the concern for preserving an unadulterated male bloodline.

Tang (2016), pp. 86-87

A wife answers a stranger who offers her gold by the roadside:

> I don't want your money! But I do want you to put an end to these inappropriate proposals! I have absolutely no intention of engaging in debauchery

Lienü zhuan 5.9, tr. Kinney (2014)

The vice is a wife's illicit sexual conduct, or deliberately pursuing it. Resistance is refusing or cutting off such approaches, especially when they are tempting or profitable. Non-sexual wrongs are outside this item.

Do not widen the English 'immorality' to lying, cruelty or theft. The bloodline concern explains the ground; it is not a required element. Do not substitute modern sexual ethics in either direction. These are historical patrilineal grounds for sending a wife away, reported rather than endorsed; whether divorce was warranted is not judged.

## Jealousy

Typical Women of China, preface and Woman's Virtues ch. 37: a wife's jealousy of her husband's concubines or other wives in a polygynous household.

The compiler's preface (the scan reads 'nndutifulness'):

> There are seven causes for which a wife may be divorced, viz., nndutifulness towards her husband's parents; having no son; immorality; jealousy ; having a leprous disease ; talkativeness ; stealing.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> There is nothing meaner on the wife's part than jealousy [of the concubines]. The moon and stars are both bright, why seek to obscure the lesser glory ? ... all should be pure, modest, and gentle, not enslaved by selfish desires, and not trying to shade the merits of their inferiors.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues ch. 37, tr. Safford (1891)

A wife, urged to go home because her absent husband has taken another wife, replies:

> If through an exacting love she opposes her husband's pleasure, can she be a true wife? ... My husband is a scholar, and, if he has two wives, is not this right? And jealousy is the chief of the seven causes for which a wife may be divorced.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues ch. 43, tr. Safford (1891)

A modern historian summarizing the ritual commentaries:

> A jealous wife may be divorced because she may pose a challenge to the institution of concubinage considered necessary for the multiplication of male offspring.

Tang (2016), pp. 86-87

The vice is a wife's resentment of her husband's concubines or secondary wives, shown by obstructing, belittling or hiding their merits. Resistance is setting that feeling aside: treating them fairly, crediting them, or accepting the arrangement.

These sources treat this ground as jealousy of the husband's concubines or other wives; ordinary envy of neighbours is not shown to fall under it. The bracket '[of the concubines]' is the translator's gloss. The acceptance of concubinage is the source's, reported not endorsed. These are historical patrilineal grounds for sending a wife away, reported rather than endorsed; whether divorce was warranted is not judged.

## Stealing

Typical Women of China, preface: stealing, last of the seven causes. Theft as such falls under it; the Li Ki's household rules and the commentaries add a wife's taking or diverting family goods.

The compiler's preface (the scan reads 'nndutifulness'):

> There are seven causes for which a wife may be divorced, viz., nndutifulness towards her husband's parents; having no son; immorality; jealousy ; having a leprous disease ; talkativeness ; stealing.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> A son and his wife should have no private goods, nor animals, nor vessels; they should not presume to borrow from, or give anything to, another person. If any one give the wife an article of food or dress, ... she should receive and offer it to her parents-in-law. ... If she want to give it to some of her own cousins, she must ask leave to do so, and that being granted, she will give it.

Li Ki, Nei Ze I.19, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

A modern historian summarizing the ritual commentaries:

> And finally theft is not only unethical, but is against the patrilineal belief that the family property should be held by the head of the family and individuals must not possess private property. It is also out of the concern that the wife may steal money and goods to give to her own relatives.

Tang (2016), pp. 86-87

The ground is stealing. Tang notes it was condemned not only as theft but because family property belonged under the family head, with a particular worry about goods passing to the wife's own relatives. So taking, keeping or giving away household goods without leave also shows it. The Li Ki's rule is openness: gifts go to the parents-in-law, and giving to her own kin is done with leave.

Open giving with permission is not stealing. The sources do not settle the status of a dowry or personal property; mark such cases uncertain. These are historical patrilineal grounds for sending a wife away, reported rather than endorsed; whether divorce was warranted is not judged.

## Talkativeness

Typical Women of China, preface: talkativeness, sixth of the seven causes. In the sources its clearest form is tale-bearing talk that sets kin against each other.

The compiler's preface (the scan reads 'nndutifulness'):

> There are seven causes for which a wife may be divorced, viz., nndutifulness towards her husband's parents; having no son; immorality; jealousy ; having a leprous disease ; talkativeness ; stealing.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

A modern historian summarizing the ritual commentaries:

> A gossipy wife may be divorced because she can cause discord among family members, especially in an extended multigenerational family.

Tang (2016), pp. 86-87

> "The great facility in starting unpleasant talk is due to the stupid maids and concubines. They gossip from one wife and sister-in-law to another, especially to the principal wife, making remarks about each lady, often uncomplimentary, under pretence of devotion to the mistress of the household. If she refuse to listen they will not dare to come a second time: but if she listens, ... until the whole household is in contention

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues ch. 25, tr. Safford (1891)

> There are women who, when this wife has angered them by some slight offence, go to their parents and with long tongues tell the affair, [happy to do so] talk against the offender's character generally, and so draw down upon her anger and severe rebuke.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues ch. 34, tr. Safford (1891)

An ode the book quotes, in Legge's translation:

> A woman with a long tongue Is [like] a stepping-stone to disorder

Book of Odes as quoted in Typical Women of China, Woman's Words ch. 76 (Legge's translation)

The ground is loose, unrestrained talk. The book's teaching and Tang's summary dwell on talk that carries tales, stirs grievances and divides the household, which shows it most clearly. Resistance is refusing to carry or listen to such tales, especially when tempted. Honest counsel to one's husband is a separate, praised duty.

Ordinary household conversation is weak evidence either way, and justified correction is not the vice. A quarrel need not follow; talk that plainly risks discord is enough. These are historical patrilineal grounds for sending a wife away, reported rather than endorsed; whether divorce was warranted is not judged.

## Undutifulness towards her husband's parents

Typical Women of China, preface: the first of the seven causes for which a wife may be divorced. The Li Ki's rules for daughters-in-law show the service whose failure it names.

The compiler's preface (the scan reads 'nndutifulness'):

> There are seven causes for which a wife may be divorced, viz., nndutifulness towards her husband's parents; having no son; immorality; jealousy ; having a leprous disease ; talkativeness ; stealing.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

Legge's note on the Li Ki cites the older list:

> the 'seven grounds of divorce,' the first of them being the wife's 'want of accordance with her husband's parents.'

Legge, note on Li Ki Nei Ze I.16 (SBE 27)

> (Sons') wives should serve their parents-in-law as they served their own. ... On getting to where they are, with bated breath and gentle voice, they should ask if their clothes are (too) warm or (too) cold, whether they are ill or pained, or uncomfortable in any part ... All this they will do with an appearance of pleasure to make their parents feel at ease.

Li Ki, Nei Ze I.3-4, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

A modern historian summarizing the ritual commentaries:

> Among the responsibilities expected of a married wife, caring for her parents-in-law ... comes first, and thus bushun fumu is a ground for divorce.

Tang (2016), pp. 86-87

The translator's summary of the book's chapter 12:

> "If you bend your will to obey the orders of your mother-in-law, she will be pleased, but you will have only performed your duty. If she makes you eat bitterness, do not forget this, and even if she is cruel and oppressive, do not hate her."

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues chs. 11-12, Safford's summary (1891)

The vice is a wife's failure in the service and deference owed to her husband's parents: neglecting their needs, defying their direction, or refusing a daughter-in-law's role. Resistance is attentive, willing care, especially when it is costly or the in-laws are hard to please.

Concerns the husband's parents, not her own. The sources set no severity threshold; one grumble is weak evidence. These are historical patrilineal grounds for sending a wife away, reported rather than endorsed; whether divorce was warranted is not judged.
