# Confucian: Four Virtues of women (四德 si de: virtue, words, deportment, employments)

## Woman's Deportment

Typical Women of China, Woman's Deportment chs. 1-8: a woman's bearing toward parents, parents-in-law and husband, respectful, pleasant and modest.

> [Girls] should learn about Woman's Virtues, Woman's Words, Woman's Deportment, and Woman's Employments.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> "In the presence of her parents or parents-in-law, a woman may not dare to sneeze or cough, neither to stretch, yawn, or loll about when tired, nor may she presume to stare at them." "**She should wear a happy face and a mild, pleasant deportment in serving them, in order to soothe them.**" "The deportment with which one should [serve] parents [must be prompted by] the strongest desire to make them happy. If a daughter have an abrupt, imperious manner, uses hasty words, carries a disagreeable look, although she daily nourished her parents with the three kinds of animal flesh, she cannot be counted filial in deportment.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Deportment chs. 1-8, tr. Safford (1891)

> [A reverential deportment towards the husband] consists in a woman living in purity, never listening to improper words, never looking at improper things. She will not use meretricious arts, nor be extravagant in ornament. She will not collect a lot of women together to gossip, nor spy at [peep out of] the public doorway.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Deportment chs. 1-8, tr. Safford (1891)

Deportment is outward bearing in service: a pleased, gentle face, quiet manner and unhasty words before parents and in-laws, and modest dress and conduct toward the husband. The book says rich food served with an imperious manner or hasty words is not filial deportment, and counts gathering women to gossip against deportment toward a husband.

Limits: Concerns manner and bearing in service. It overlaps with Words, since hasty words and gossip are named here too. Minute etiquette, such as not sneezing before elders, is a historical example of respect. This is historical teaching of female subordination; score it as the source teaches it, not as a modern judgment.

## Woman's Employments

Typical Women of China, Woman's Employments ch. 1, with the definition it attributes to the lady Tsao (Ban Zhao): diligent household work, namely silkworms, spinning, wine and food, and receiving guests.

> [Girls] should learn about Woman's Virtues, Woman's Words, Woman's Deportment, and Woman's Employments.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> The lady Tsao has said: "Woman's Employments do not call for extraordinary skill. Minding silk-worms, spinning thread, preparing wine and food, and attending to guests, these things constitute woman's work

Typical Women of China, Woman's Employments ch. 1, tr. Safford (1891)

> and could carry on all the work in silk and linen, making cloth and silken fabrics, and maintaining a watchful care over the various stores and depositories (of the household).

Li Ki, Hun Yi 8-10, tr. Legge (SBE 28)

> Besides, it is the occupation of woman to spin silk and twist hempen thread. From the Empress down, if one woman is idle, it is because she is proud and lazy.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Employments ch. 15, tr. Safford (1891)

Employments is diligent, ordinary household work: textiles, food and wine, care of stores and hospitality. Extraordinary skill is not required. In one tale the book gives, a widow rejects her son's plea that his ample salary makes her toil needless, tells him to share it with poor relatives, and says an idle woman is proud and lazy.

Limits: Concerns diligent ordinary work. It overlaps with the other studies: the book ties idleness to pride and laziness. The division of labour by sex is historical. This is historical teaching of female subordination; score it as the source teaches it, not as a modern judgment.

## Woman's Virtues

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues ch. 1, with the definition it attributes to the lady Ts'ao (Ban Zhao): the first of the four studies set for girls, from the Li Ki's 'virtue, speech, carriage and work of a wife'.

> [Girls] should learn about Woman's Virtues, Woman's Words, Woman's Deportment, and Woman's Employments.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> she was taught there the virtue, the speech, the carriage, and the work of a wife.

Li Ki, Hun Yi 8-10, tr. Legge (SBE 28)

> [Woman's] virtues, says the lady Ts'ao, are not of a conspicuous or brilliant order. They are purity, refusal of a second marriage if her betrothed or her husband should die, the right [government] of her household, the practice of modesty and humility, and the regulation of life by the rules of propriety.

Typical Women of China, Woman's Virtues ch. 1, tr. Safford (1891)

> Be modest and respectful in demeanor. Prefer others to yourself. If you have done good, do not proclaim it; if evil, do not excuse it.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> Though a woman had elegant deportment, fascinating manners, eminent gifts, was eloquent in speech and perfect in beauty, yet lacked virtue, and wielded power beyond her sphere, she might subvert a city, and her words might overthrow a kingdom.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

Woman's Virtues is the moral core of the four studies: chastity, refusing remarriage after a betrothed's or husband's death, ordering the household well, modesty and humility, and living by propriety. The preface ranks it above grace, talent or eloquence. The studies overlap: the same chapter makes respectfully preparing food part of serving a husband, and the book praises a widow who supports her mother-in-law by spinning and weaving.

Limits: The rule against remarriage is the book's historical standard and is objectionable today; score it as taught. One act may show one part of the definition without the others. Work or manner can show this virtue when the scene supplies household government, humility or propriety. The definition comes through Safford, who says she often paraphrases. This is historical teaching of female subordination; score it as the source teaches it, not as a modern judgment.

## Woman's Words

Typical Women of China, Woman's Words ch. 2: a wife's speech kept within the household, and truthful counsel to her husband.

> [Girls] should learn about Woman's Virtues, Woman's Words, Woman's Deportment, and Woman's Employments.

Typical Women of China, Chinese Author's Preface, tr. Safford (1891)

> The words of a wife should not pass outside the doors of the inner apartments ; her husband is the only person with whom she may at all times converse familiarly. A faithful minister, if his monarch does wrong reproves him ; a virtuous wife, in the same way, tells her husband when he is in fault, and aids him to do better. **A really superior man does not desire flattery from his wife, but her true words are precious.**

Typical Women of China, Woman's Words ch. 2, tr. Safford (1891)

> A woman with a long tongue Is [like] a stepping-stone to disorder

Book of Odes as quoted in Typical Women of China, Woman's Words ch. 76 (Legge's translation)

Woman's Words asks for reserve and truth: a wife's words do not pass outside the inner apartments, and she tells her husband plainly of his faults instead of flattering him. Such correction is part of the virtue, not a breach of it.

Limits: Ordinary household instructions neither show nor breach it. Tale-bearing that divides a family is also a separate ground for divorce. This is historical teaching of female subordination; score it as the source teaches it, not as a modern judgment.
