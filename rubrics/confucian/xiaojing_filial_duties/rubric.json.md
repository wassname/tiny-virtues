# Confucian: Five duties of a filial son and three things a son ought not to do (Classic of Filial Piety, ch. 10)

## Acting against the natural feeling of the public

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10: the third undoing fault, in Chen's wording, which the label follows: acting against the natural feeling of the public. Legge's parallel translation of the same clause reads quarrelsomeness among one's equals.

> A son ... ought not to act against the natural feeling of the public. ... if he does anything contrary to the public feeling, he will probably be the object of attacks.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> and among his equals, will not be quarrelsome. ... among equals quarrelsomeness leads to the wielding of weapons.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

The Li Ki gives a matching rule:

> For all sons it is the rule:— ... and, when with their companions, not to quarrel.

Li Ki, Qu Li I.ii.2-3, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

> The student of virtue has no contentions. If it be said he cannot avoid them, shall this be in archery? But he bows complaisantly to his competitors

Analects 3.7, tr. Legge

> Our body and hair and skin are all derived from our parents, and therefore we have no right to injure any of them in the least. This is the first duty of a child.

Xiaojing ch. 1, tr. Chen (1908)

In Chen's wording the fault is doing what is contrary to the natural feeling of the public, which he warns invites attack. Legge's parallel reading, matched by the Li Ki rule, is quarrelling among one's equals, one clear form of it. The two translations are readings of one clause, not two tests. Ch. 10 says a son who so wrongs himself cannot be counted filial, and ch. 1 makes not injuring the body received from parents the first duty.

The label follows Chen's broader wording, so conduct that clearly goes against the public's natural feeling counts fully even without a quarrel; agreement with Legge's reading is not a measure of degree. Being quiet does not exempt such conduct. The attack Chen warns of is a consequence, not a requirement. Which translation better renders the Chinese is not settled here.

## Dissatisfaction with one's inferior position

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10: the second undoing fault, in Chen's wording, which the label follows: showing dissatisfaction with one's lower position. Legge's parallel translation of the same clause reads insubordination in a low situation.

> A son ... ought not to show dissatisfaction with his inferior position to that of others, ... if he feels dissatisfied with his lower position, he may be led to do illegal acts

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> in a low situation, will be free from insubordination; ... in a low situation insubordination leads to punishment

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> They are few who, being filial and fraternal, are fond of offending against their superiors. There have been none, who, not liking to offend against their superiors, have been fond of stirring up confusion.

Analects 1.2, tr. Legge

> To be poor without murmuring is difficult.

Analects 14.11, tr. Legge

> When the command is wrong, a son should resist his father, and a minister should resist his August Master.

Xiaojing ch. 15, tr. Chen (1908)

In Chen's wording the fault is showing dissatisfaction with one's lower place; illegal acts are a consequence he warns of, not a requirement. Legge's parallel reading, insubordination, names a form the fault can take. The two translations are readings of one clause, not two tests. Bearing a low or poor station without murmuring resists the fault. Protesting a wrong command is a separate duty, not this vice.

The label follows Chen's translation, so clearly shown dissatisfaction with one's station counts fully even without defiance or lawbreaking; agreement with Legge's reading is not a measure of degree. These passages do not address seeking advancement by proper means. Remonstrance against wrong (ch. 15) is not insubordination.

## Feeling proud of one's high position

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10: the first of three faults that undo a son's service, pride in a high position; with chs. 3 and 8 and the Li Ki.

> A son ought not to feel proud of the high position he occupies, ... If he is proud and haughty when he is a high official, he will soon bring ruin upon himself and his family; ... Having thus wronged himself, he cannot be considered as a filial son, although he treats his parents every day to luxurious meals.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

Legge's translation:

> He who (thus) serves his parents, in a high situation, will be free from pride; ... In a high situation pride leads to ruin; ... If those three things be not put away, though a son every day contribute beef, mutton, and pork to nourish his parents, he is not filial.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> Any man will be secure in his position, however high it may be, if he does not behave himself in a haughty manner

Xiaojing ch. 3, tr. Chen (1908)

> If the head of a family do not act haughtily towards his servant, he cannot act so to his wife and children.

Xiaojing ch. 8, tr. Chen (1908)

> Whenever a son, having received the three (first) gifts (of the ruler), declines (to use) the carriage and horses, the people of the hamlets ... will proclaim him filial

Li Ki, Qu Li I.ii.2-3, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

Legge's note on that rule:

> he declines the third gift from humility,—not to parade himself as superior to his father and others in his circle.

Legge, note on Li Ki Qu Li I.ii.3 (SBE 27)

The vice is arrogance bred by rank or office: haughtiness toward those below, or display above one's elders. The Classic counts it against filial piety because the ruin it brings falls on one's family; feeding parents well does not offset it.

Holding rank, office or wealth is not the vice. Pride not tied to a position one holds is outside this item.

## Making parents happy, especially at meals

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10, the second duty: in nourishing parents, aim at their pleasure and not only their food; with Analects 2.8 and Mencius 4A19.

> (2) He must try to make them happy in every possible way, especially when the meal is served.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> in his nourishing of them, his endeavour is to give them the utmost pleasure

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> Tsze-hsia asked what filial piety was. The Master said, '**The difficulty is with the countenance.** If, when their elders have any troublesome affairs, the young take the toil of them, and if, when the young have wine and food, they set them before their elders, is THIS to be considered filial piety?'

Analects 2.5-8, tr. Legge

> "Tsang-tsze, in nourishing Tsang Seih, was always sure to have spirits and flesh provided. And when they were about to be removed, he would ask respectfully to whom [what was left] should be given. ... After the death of Tsang Seih, when Tsang Yuen came to nourish [Tsang-tsze], he was sure to have spirits and flesh provided; but when the things were about to be removed, he did not ask to whom [what was left] should be given, and if [his father] asked whether there was anything left, he would answer, 'No;' — intending to bring them on again. This was what is called — 'nourishing the mouth and body.' We may call [Tsang-tsze's] practice — 'nourishing the will.'"

Mencius 4A19, tr. Legge (1875)

The duty looks to the parents' pleasure: a glad face, attention to what they would like, and, in Mencius's contrast, attending to a parent's wishes about the food as well as feeding him. Plain food brought gladly can show it; rich food served with a sour face goes against it.

Providing food is not by itself this duty, and plain food does not breach it. 'Especially when the meal is served' marks the meal as the main occasion, not the only one. Mencius contrasts Tsang Yuen's practice with his father's, the one he calls filial, without calling Tsang Yuen unfilial.

## Sacrificing to deceased parents with utmost solemnity

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10, the fifth duty: solemn reverence in the sacrifices to one's deceased parents; with chs. 16 and 18 and Analects 3.12.

> (5) He must offer sacrifices to his deceased parents with the utmost solemnity.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> in sacrificing to them, he displays the utmost solemnity.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> Why do we offer sacrifices to our ancestors in our family shrine? Because we ought not to forget them. ... If we can show respect to them when we offer them sacrifices in our family shrine, we shall be blessed by the Supreme Father and Mother.

Xiaojing ch. 16, tr. Chen (1908)

> In memory of our deceased parent we build a shrine. For the purpose of showing our remembrance we offer sacrifices every spring and autumn.

Xiaojing ch. 18, tr. Chen (1908)

> He sacrificed to the dead, as if they were present. ... The Master said, 'I consider my not being present at the sacrifice, as if I did not sacrifice.'

Analects 3.12, tr. Legge

Solemnity is reverent seriousness in the rite itself: taking part in person, attending as if the parent were present, and keeping propriety. These passages measure it by remembrance and respect, not by cost.

Concerns rites for one's own deceased parents. The promise of blessing is the Classic's claim, not a test of the act. A later tale praises carving images of dead parents to serve them as if alive; it does not say whether that service included the formal sacrifices.

## Showing great sorrow for dead parents

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10, the fourth duty, explained in ch. 18: real grief in mourning a parent, within the Classic's own limits.

> (4) He ought to show great sorrow for them when they are dead.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> in mourning for them (dead), he exhibits every demonstration of grief

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> Confucius said: "When a filial son loses his parent, he, of course, cannot help crying piteously. He cannot feel happy when he hears music. He will have no appetite for food, however tempting a savoury. ... All these tell us the extent of his sorrow for his lost parent. What is meant by the saying that he must try to eat something after three days from the death of his parent, though he has no appetite for it? It teaches us that although we have to show great sorrow for the dead, yet **we must not sacrifice ourselves on their account**, and that we must not carry self-mortification so far as to destroy our life. ... That mourning only extends to the period of three years shows that there is a limit for our sorrow.

Xiaojing ch. 18, tr. Chen (1908)

> In the ceremonies of mourning, it is better that there be deep sorrow than a minute attention to observances.

Analects 3.4, tr. Legge

> But a superior man, during the whole period of mourning, does not enjoy pleasant food which he may eat, nor derive pleasure from music which he may hear.

Analects 17.21, tr. Legge

Sorrow shows in real grief and in setting pleasures aside through the mourning period; deep sorrow matters more than exact observance. The Classic also sets limits: grief must not destroy one's life, and mourning has an end.

The three-year term and mourning dress are historical rules. Grief that endangers one's own life exceeds the Classic's limit, even though a later tale praises a son who nearly died of grief. Loud wailing for show is weak evidence of sorrow.

## Taking extra care of parents when sick

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10, the third duty: anxious, attentive care when a parent is ill; with the Li Ki rules for a son while a parent is sick.

> (3) He must take extra care of them when they are sick.

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> when they are ill, he feels the greatest anxiety

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> When his father or mother is ill, (a young man) who has been capped should not use his comb, nor walk with his elbows stuck out, nor speak on idle topics, nor take his lute or cithern in hand. ... He should not laugh so as to show his teeth, nor be angry till he breaks forth in reviling. When the [illness] is gone, he may resume his former habits.

Li Ki, Qu Li I.iv.1, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

> When a ruler is ill, and has to drink medicine, the minister first tastes it. The same is the rule for a son and an ailing parent.

Li Ki, Qu Li II, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

The duty joins inner anxiety to changed conduct: close attendance, nursing, care over medicine, and setting amusement aside while the parent is ill. When the illness passes, ordinary life resumes.

Applies while a parent is ill. The texts do not fix how much nursing a son must do with his own hands; judge the concern and care actually shown. The Li Ki's grooming and medical rules are historical forms of that concern.

## Venerating parents in daily life

Classic of Filial Piety ch. 10, the first of a son's five duties: reverence toward living parents in everyday conduct, with the Analects' contrast between reverence and mere support.

> Confucius said: "A filial son has five duties to perform to his parents: (1) **He must venerate them in daily life.** ..."

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Chen (1908)

James Legge's translation of the same clause:

> In his general conduct to them, he manifests the utmost reverence

Xiaojing ch. 10, tr. Legge (SBE 3, 1899)

> Tsze-yu asked what filial piety was. The Master said, 'The filial piety of now-a-days means the support of one's parents. But dogs and horses likewise are able to do something in the way of support;-- **without reverence, what is there to distinguish the one support given from the other?**'

Analects 2.5-8, tr. Legge

> For all sons it is the rule:—in winter, to warm (the bed for their parents), and to cool it in summer; in the evening, to adjust everything (for their repose), and to inquire (about their health) in the morning

Li Ki, Qu Li I.ii.2-3, tr. Legge (SBE 27)

> In serving his parents, a son may remonstrate with them, but gently; when he sees that they do not incline to follow his advice, he shows an increased degree of reverence, but does not abandon his purpose

Analects 4.18, tr. Legge

> When the command is wrong, a son should resist his father, and a minister should resist his August Master.

Xiaojing ch. 15, tr. Chen (1908)

Reverence is the spirit of everyday service: attentive care for the parents' comfort and a respectful manner toward them. Feeding or housing parents without respect is support, not this duty. Gently declining a wrong command, while showing more reverence, does not conflict with it.

Concerns one's own living parents; courtesy to other elders is a related extension, not this item. The Classic does not require obeying a wrong command. Later tales' miraculous rewards are not part of the duty.
