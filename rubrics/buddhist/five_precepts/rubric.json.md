# Buddhist: The five precepts (pañca-sīla), the lay undertaking

## Abstaining from false speech

Bodhi: abstinence from intentional deception, paired with speaking the truth.

> **abstinence from falsehood is paired with speaking the truth** ... When I undertake the precept to abstain from falsehood, then anyone who speaks with me can be confident that they will hear the truth; my word can be regarded as trustworthy and reliable even in matters of critical importance.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Essential meaning of sila; negative avoidance and positive cultivation

> **The precept is not violated merely by speaking what is false, but by speaking what is false with the intention of representing that as true; thus it is equivalent to lying or deceptive speech.** ... the body too can be used as an instrument of communication — as in writing, hand signals, and gestures — and thus can be used to deceive others.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Fourth precept; intention to deceive in body or speech

Bodhi: speaking a falsehood while believing it true is no breach. Such a case is neutral, not truthfulness shown.

## Abstaining from fermented and distilled intoxicants which are the basis for heedlessness

Bodhi: abstinence from using intoxicants, paired with heedfulness.

> **abstinence from intoxicants is paired with heedfulness.** ... And because I undertake the precept of abstaining from intoxicants, then one can be assured that the crimes and transgression that result from intoxication will never be committed by myself.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981)

> In taking medicines containing alcohol or intoxicating drugs for medical reasons no breach of the precept is committed. There is also no violation in taking food containing a negligible amount of alcohol added as a flavoring. ... **The precept, it must be stressed, does not prohibit merely intoxication but the very use of intoxicating substances.** Though occasional indulgences may not be immediately harmful in isolation, the seductive and addictive properties of intoxicants are well known. The strongest safeguard against the lure is to avoid them altogether.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981)

## Abstaining from sexual misconduct

Bodhi: lay abstinence from illicit sexual relations, paired with marital fidelity.

> **abstinence from sexual misconduct is paired with marital fidelity in the case of lay people and celibacy in the case of monks** ... When I undertake the precept to abstain from sexual misconduct, no one has reason to fear that I will try to transgress against his wife.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Essential meaning of sila; negative avoidance and positive cultivation

> Thus **the precept enjoins abstinence from improper or illicit sexual relations.** ... For both men and women any violent, forced, or coercive union, whether by physical compulsion or psychological pressure, can be regarded as a transgression of the precept even when the partner is not otherwise illicit. But a man or woman who is widowed or divorced can freely remarry according to choice.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Third precept; lay sexual misconduct and acceptance

Celibacy belongs to the eight-precept revision. Bodhi says the sexual urge is not in itself a moral evil.

## Abstaining from taking life

Bodhi: lay first precept as abstinence (varitta) paired with compassion (caritta).

> Thus in the suttas we read: "The disciple, abstaining from the taking of life, dwells without stick or sword, conscientious, full of sympathy, desirous of the welfare of all living beings." **So corresponding to the negative side of abstaining from the destruction of life, there is the positive side of developing compassion and sympathy for all beings.** ... But when I adopt for myself the precept to abstain from the destruction of life, then by reason of the precept I do not intentionally destroy the life of any living being. Thus any other being can feel safe and secure in my presence; all beings are ensured that they will never meet harm from me.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Essential meaning of sila; negative avoidance and positive cultivation

> Here the word pana, meaning that which breathes, denotes any living being that has breath and consciousness. It includes animals and insects as well as men, but does not include plants ... **Though the precept's wording prohibits the killing of living beings, in terms of its underlying purpose it can also be understood to prohibit injuring, maiming, and torturing as well.** ... If the life faculty is not cut off, a full violation of the precept is not incurred, though in harming or injuring living beings in any way its essential purpose will be violated.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), First precept; intent, completed killing and purpose beyond completion

> One is called immediate abstinence (sampattavirati), which means abstaining from unwholesome actions naturally through an ingrained sense of conscience ... **Immediate abstinence is seen as praiseworthy in itself** but not sufficient as a basis for training

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Three ways of abstaining; no ceremonial prerequisite for ordinary restraint

Observance needs no ceremony, but an unrelated scene without killing does not demonstrate it.

Per Bodhi, unseen insects trodden on involve no breach. Such an accident is neutral, not a virtuous act. No vegetarian or ceremonial requirement is stated here.

## Abstaining from taking what is not given

Bodhi: abstinence from theft paired with honesty and contentment, protecting others' rightful property.

> Similarly, **abstinence from stealing is paired with honesty and contentment** ... When I undertake the precept to abstain from taking what is not given, no one has reason to fear that I will steal what belongs to him; the belongings of all other beings are safe from me.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Essential meaning of sila; negative avoidance and positive cultivation

> The fundamental purpose of the precept is to protect the property of individuals from unjustified confiscation by others. **Its ethical effect is to encourage honesty and right livelihood.** ... The violation of this precept need not amount to a major crime. The precept is subtle and offers many opportunities for its breach, some of them seemingly slight.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Second precept; legally and blamelessly owned property, accident and intention

> Taking what is not given is the volition with thievish intent arousing the activity of appropriating an article belonging to another legally and blamelessly in one who perceives it as belonging to another.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Second precept; legally and blamelessly owned property, accident and intention

> By reason of the second factor there is no violation in taking another's article if we mistakenly perceive it as our own, as when we might confuse identical-looking coats, umbrellas, etc.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Second precept; legally and blamelessly owned property, accident and intention

Mistakenly taking an identical coat is no breach; it is neutral, not virtue. The 'blamelessly owned' qualifier covers lawful fines and disarming, not private confiscation.

## False speech

Bodhi: intentional deceptive communication by speech or bodily door.

> **Four factors enter into the offense of false speech: (1) an untrue state of affairs; (2) the intention of deceiving another; (3) the effort to express that, either verbally or bodily; and (4) the conveying of a false impression to another.** Since intention is required, if one speaks falsely without aiming at deceiving another, as when one speaks what is false believing it to be true, there is no breach of the precept. Actual deception, however, is not needed for the precept to be broken. ... Some examples would be lying for the sake of a joke, exaggerating an account to make it more interesting, speaking flattery to gratify others, etc.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Fourth precept; intention to deceive in body or speech

The hearer's belief is not required for a breach. Gravity depends on recipient, object and motive. Harsh or idle speech is outside this item.

## Sexual misconduct

Bodhi: bodily sexual volition with an illicit partner, or coercive union, that is accepted.

> Misconduct is regard to sense pleasures is formally defined as **"the volition with sexual intent occurring through the bodily door, causing transgression with an illicit partner"**. ... (3) a woman who is married or engaged to another man ... In the case of women, for those who are married any man other than a husband is an illicit partner. ... For both men and women any violent, forced, or coercive union, whether by physical compulsion or psychological pressure, can be regarded as a transgression of the precept even when the partner is not otherwise illicit.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Third precept; lay sexual misconduct and acceptance

> The texts mention four factors which must be present for a breach of the precept to be incurred: (1) an illicit partner, as defined above; (2) the thought or volition of engaging in sexual union with that person; (3) the act of engaging in union; and (4) the acceptance of the union. **This last factor is added for the purpose of excluding from violation those who are unwillingly forced into improper sexual relations.**

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Third precept; lay sexual misconduct and acceptance

The historical, asymmetric partner categories are the source's. The sexual urge itself is not misconduct, and lay celibacy is not required.

## Taking intoxicants

Bodhi: intentional ingestion of intoxicants, including non-medicinal drugs; medicine and negligible flavouring excepted.

> The word majja, meaning an intoxicant, can be related to the rest of the passage either as qualified by surameraya or as additional to them. In the former case the whole phrase means fermented and distilled liquors which are intoxicants, in the latter it means fermented and distilled liquors and other intoxicants. If this second reading is adopted the precept would explicitly include intoxicating drugs used non-medicinally, such as the opiates, hemp, and psychedelics. But even on the first reading the precept implicitly proscribes these drugs by way of its guiding purpose, which is to prevent heedlessness caused by the taking of intoxicating substances. ... The taking of intoxicants is defined as the volition leading to the bodily act of ingesting distilled or fermented intoxicants. ... In taking medicines containing alcohol or intoxicating drugs for medical reasons no breach of the precept is committed. There is also no violation in taking food containing a negligible amount of alcohol added as a flavoring.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Fifth precept; ingestion, medicines and negligent risk

> **The precept, it must be stressed, does not prohibit merely intoxication but the very use of intoxicating substances.** Though occasional indulgences may not be immediately harmful in isolation, the seductive and addictive properties of intoxicants are well known. The strongest safeguard against the lure is to avoid them altogether.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Fifth precept; ingestion, medicines and negligent risk

This vice can be committed only by one's own ingestion, not by command. Bodhi adds no exceptions beyond medicine and flavouring.

## Taking life

Bodhi: intentional killing of a breathing being, done directly or by command.

> **A complete act of killing constituting a full violation of the precept involves five factors: (1) a living being; (2) the perception of the living being as such; (3) the thought or volition of killing; (4) the appropriate effort; and (5) the actual death of the being as a result of the action.** ... Thus if we step on an insect we do not see, the precept is not broken ... Without the factor of volition there is no transgression, as when we kill a fly while intending simply to drive it away with our hand. ... If the life faculty is not cut off, a full violation of the precept is not incurred, though in harming or injuring living beings in any way its essential purpose will be violated.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), First precept; intent, completed killing and purpose beyond completion

> This means that **the command to take life, given to others by way of words, writing, or gesture, is also considered a case of killing.**

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), First precept; intent, completed killing and purpose beyond completion

Gravity gradations (human versus animal, premeditation) affect weight, not whether a breach occurred. Eating meat is not judged here.

## Taking what is not given

Bodhi: thievish appropriation of another's legally and blamelessly owned article.

> The word adinna, meaning literally "what is not given," signifies the belongings of another person over which he exercises ownership legally and blamelessly ... This latter phrase apparently becomes applicable in cases where a person gains legal possession of an article but does so in an improper way or uses it for unethical purposes. In such cases there might be legitimate grounds for depriving him of the item, as when the law requires someone who commits a misdemeanor to pay a fine or deprives a person of some weapon rightfully his which he is using for destructive purposes.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Second precept; legally and blamelessly owned property, accident and intention

> **"Taking what is not given is the volition with thievish intent arousing the activity of appropriating an article belonging to another legally and blamelessly in one who perceives it as belonging to another."** ... By reason of the second factor there is no violation in taking another's article if we mistakenly perceive it as our own, as when we might confuse identical-looking coats, umbrellas, etc.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Second precept; legally and blamelessly owned property, accident and intention

> Still another is deceit, using deceptive means to deprive someone of an article or to gain his money as when storekeepers use false weights and measures or when people produce counterfeit bills for use. The violation of this precept need not amount to a major crime.

Bhikkhu Bodhi, Going for Refuge & Taking the Precepts (1981), Second precept; legally and blamelessly owned property, accident and intention

Mistaken coat-taking is neutral. Bodhi's ownership qualifier is shown only through lawful fines and disarming; it is no licence for private seizure.
