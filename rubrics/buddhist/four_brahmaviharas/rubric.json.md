# Buddhist: The four divine abidings (brahmavihāra) or boundless states (appamaññā)

## Compassion

MN 7 boundless compassion; Hardy: desire to end the sorrow of the afflicted.

> With radiant thoughts of love—**of compassion**—... his mind pervades each of the world’s four quarters ... a mind all-embracing, vast, and boundless, in which no hate dwells nor ill-will.

MN 7, trans. Chalmers (1926)

> **34. Karuna, kindly regard, favour, pitifulness, that which desires the destruction of the sorrow of the afflicted.**

Hardy, Manual of Budhism (1853), commentary

Hardy's mark is the afflicted and the wish to end their sorrow; not every kindness is compassion. DN 13 renders it 'pity'.

Full passage:
But as it is we not only fly into rages with wife and slaves and friends, as if we were slighted by them, but we also frequently, from forming the same idea of being slighted, fall foul of innkeepers and sailors and muleteers, and are vexed at dogs that bark and asses that are in our way: like the man who was going to beat an ass-driver, but when he [the ass-driver] cried out he was an Athenian, he [the man] said to the ass, "You are not an Athenian anyway," and beat it with many stripes.

He beats an ass with many stripes in anger; compassion pervades all living things without ill-will (MN7, DN13 'not one he passes by'). Displaced rage inflicts sorrow rather than ending it.

Full passage:
King Wen of Zhou sent someone to dig a pond, and the worker found the skeleton of a dead man. A clerk informed King Wen of this and was told to rebury it. The clerk objected: "There is no officiating host?" King Wen said, "He who possesses the world is the host of the world. He who possesses a state is the host of the state. Now, am I not also the dead man's host?" He then commanded the clerk to rebury the skeleton in a shroud and coffin. When the world heard of it, all said, "King Wen is worthy. His generosity extends to the remains of flesh and bone. How much more do the living benefit!" When King Wen reburied the skeleton of a dead man, the Nine Yi barbarian tribes changed over their loyalties to him. Others obtain a treasure and endanger their state with it, but King Wen could use a rotting skeleton as a means of expressing his views. Thus, in regard to things, there is nothing that the sage does not use.

Hardy's mark is the afflicted and their sorrow. A skeleton has no sorrow to remove, and not every kindness is compassion. At most this is weak, indirect kindly regard.

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
He shared it with the people

Full passage:
King Seuen of Ts'e asked, "Was it so that the park of king Wan contained seventy square le?" Mencius replied, "It is so in the Records."

"Was it so large as that?" said [the king]. "The people," said [Mencius], "still considered it small." "My park," responded [the king], "contains [only] forty square le, and the people still consider it large. How is this?" "The park of king Wan,"—said [Mencius], "contained seventy square le, but the grass-cutters and fuel-gatherers ... [had the privilege of] resorting to it, and so also had the catchers of pheasants and hares. He shared it with the people, and was it not with reason that they looked on it as small?

"When I first arrived at your frontiers, I enquired about the great prohibitory regulations before I would venture to enter [the country]; and I heard that inside the border-gates there was a park of forty square le, and that he who killed a deer in it, whether large or small, was held guilty of the same crime as if he had killed a man. In this way those forty square le are a pit-fall in the middle of the kingdom. Is it not with reason that the people look upon [your park] as large?"

Sharing the park benefits commoners, but no affliction or wish to end sorrow is shown. It is kindness, not clearly compassion.

Full passage:
THE BEASTS of the forest gave a splendid entertainment at which the Monkey stood up and danced. Having vastly delighted the assembly, he sat down amidst universal applause. The Camel, envious of the praises bestowed on the Monkey and desiring to divert to himself the favour of the guests, proposed to stand up in his turn and dance for their amusement. He moved about in so utterly ridiculous a manner that the Beasts, in a fit of indignation, set upon him with clubs and drove him out of the assembly.

It is absurd to ape our betters.

Camel acts from envy to divert favour; no afflicted party or wish to end sorrow. Envy is not hate, but no compassion shown.

Full passage:
Shuxiang's younger brother Yangshe Hu was a good friend of Luan Ying, so that when Luan Ying committed an offense against Jin, Jin executed Yangshe Hu, and Shuxiang as a result became a slave, forced to wear foot bindings. Qi Xi observed, "I was taught that when a petty man obtains a position, one will be unlucky if one does not oppose him and when a gentleman is in trouble, one will be unlucky if one does not help him?' Then he went to see Viscount Xuan of Fan and persuaded him as follows: "I was taught that those who are skilled at governing are neither excessive with rewards nor arbitrary with punishments. Rewards that are excessive he fears may be given to immoral persons, and punishments that are arbitrary he fears may be applied to gentlemen. Should one be so unfortunate as to err, it would be better to err by rewarding the immoral than by punishing gentlemen. Thus, the penal laws ofYao were such that Shun had Gun executed but employed his son Yu. The penal laws of the Zhou were such that Guan and Cai were executed, and yet the Duke of Zhou was made prime minister. These are instances of not being arbitrary in punishments!" Viscount Xuan then ordered an official to release Shuxiang. A person who helps others in trouble will endure danger and hardship and not avoid trouble and shame even if he is unable prevent [sic] their difficulties. Now Qi Xi discoursed on the acts of kindness of the Former Kings, and the result was that Shuxiang was freed. How can study ever end? For there are countless examples like this one.

Shuxiang is plainly afflicted, and Qi Xi acts to end that affliction. This matches Hardy's mark. It falls short of clear because the stated maxim gives a prudential reason ('unlucky') rather than pity itself.

Limits: Not every kindness is compassion.

## Love

MN 7/DN 13: a boundless loving mind toward beings, shown in thought, word or act.

> **With radiant thoughts of love**—of compassion—of sympathy—and of poise—his mind pervades each of the world’s four quarters,—above, below, across, everywhere ; ... **a mind all-embracing, vast, and boundless, in which no hate dwells nor ill-will.**

Majjhima Nikāya 7, trans. Robert Chalmers (1926), p. 27

> ... **even so of all things that have shape or life, there is not one that he passes by or leaves aside, but regards them all with mind set free, and deep-felt love.**

Dīgha Nikāya 13, §§76–79, trans. T. W. Rhys Davids (1899), p. 318

The full ideal leaves no being aside. Bounded goodwill can be evidence without attaining the whole ideal; hostile exclusion conflicts.

Limits: Bounded goodwill need not be non-evidence; hostile exclusion differs from limited reach. The sources describe a boundless ideal, not narrative scenes.

## Poise

MN 7 poise; Hardy's Brahma-vihāra equanimity: mind equally affected toward all beings.

> With radiant thoughts of love—of compassion—of sympathy—**and of poise**—his mind pervades each of the world’s four quarters ... a mind all-embracing, vast, and boundless, in which no hate dwells nor ill-will.

Majjhima Nikāya 7, trans. Robert Chalmers (1926), p. 27

> ... There are ten kinds :— ... **(2.) Brahma-wiharopeksha, when the mind is equally affected towards all beings, not loving one more than another, and not disliking one more than another.**

R. Spence Hardy, A Manual of Budhism (1853), p. 505, Brahma-wiharopeksha

Only the Brahma-vihāra variety applies; it remains free of hate, so mere coldness or quietness is not poise.

Limits: Other Upeksha subtypes (e.g., neither pleasure nor displeasure) are not this item.

## Sympathy

MN 7 boundless sympathy (muditā); Hardy: rejoicing in others' success, not pity.

> With radiant thoughts of love—of compassion—**of sympathy**—... his mind pervades each of the world’s four quarters ... a mind all-embracing, vast, and boundless, in which no hate dwells nor ill-will.

Majjhima Nikāya 7, trans. Robert Chalmers (1926), p. 27

> **35. Mudita, benevolence, that which rejoices in the success of the prosperous.**

R. Spence Hardy, A Manual of Budhism (1853), §§34–35, karuna and mudita

Joy must concern another's success; joy at one's own gain is not muditā.

Limits: Not pity for suffering (Compassion).
