# Buddhist: The fourteen evil things of the Sigālovāda (four vices of conduct, four motives, six channels for dissipating wealth)

## Associating with evil companions

Taking gamblers, cheats, tipplers or the violent as one's companions.

> Six, young householder, are the perils from associating with evil companions : **any gambler, any libertine, any tippler, any cheat, any swindler, any man of violence is his friend and companion.**

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

The source concerns companionship. Ordinary passing contact with such people is not shown here to be the vice.

## Being addicted to intoxicating liquors

Addiction to liquor as a channel for dissipating wealth, with six named dangers.

> There are, young householder, **these six dangers through the being addicted to intoxicating liquors : — actual loss of wealth, increase of quarrels, susceptibility to disease, loss of good character, ... indecent exposure, impaired intelligence.**

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

The source targets addiction.

## Being infatuated by gambling

Infatuation with gambling, with six named perils.

> Six, young householder, are the perils for him who is infatuated with gambling : **as winner he begets hatred ; when beaten he mourns his lost wealth** ; ... his actual substance is wasted ; his word has no weight in a court of law ; he is despised by friends and officials

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

The marriage-prospect peril reflects historical norms and is attributed, not endorsed.

## Destruction of life

DN31 names killing as a vice of conduct; the MN41 gloss supplies the hunter image.

> What are the four vices of conduct that he has put away ? **The destruction of life**, the taking what is not given, licentiousness, and lying speech.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

> As regards bodily unrighteousness, a man (1) **may take life,—as a hunter with hands bathed in blood, given to killing and slaying, merciless to living creatures**

MN41,Chalmers(1926),pp203–204

DN31 only names the act. The descriptive gloss comes from the separate discourse MN41.

Counting intended killing only, and the treatment of meat-eating, are not specified by DN31. The source's rebirth claims are attributed, not asserted.

## Enmity

An evil deed done from enmity or hate. It is not mere felt anger.

> **Evil deeds are done from motives of partiality, enmity, stupidity and fear.** But inasmuch as the Ariyan disciple is not led away by these motives, he through them does no evil deed.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

The term is unexplained beyond this list and verse. Felt anger without an evil deed is not established as this vice.

## Fear

An evil deed done from fear. It is not every frightened act.

> **Evil deeds are done from motives of partiality, enmity, stupidity and fear.** But inasmuch as the Ariyan disciple is not led away by these motives, he through them does no evil deed.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

The qualifier is an evil deed done from fear. Fear alone is not this vice.

## Frequenting the streets at unseemly hours

Habitually roaming at unseemly hours. It is not every late errand.

> Six, young householder, are the perils from frequenting the streets at unseemly hours : — **he himself is without guard or protection and so also are wife and children ; so also is his property** ; he moreover becomes suspected [as the doer] of [undiscovered] crimes ... and false rumours fix on him, and many are the troubles he goes out to meet.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

'Unseemly hours' is not defined in the source. The footnote on fathered crimes is translator commentary.

## Haunting fairs

A restless pursuit of shows. It is not every festival visit.

> Six, young householder, are the perils from the haunting of fairs : — **[He is ever thinking] where is there dancing ? where is there singing ?** ...

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

Gap: the 'perils' listed are only these restless questions. No separate consequences are given.

Do not treat every social gathering as this vice. The source's own wording stresses compulsive frequency.

## Licentiousness

DN31 names licentiousness, and its verse says 'adultery'. The MN41 gloss specifies girls under guardianship or betrothed.

> The destruction of life, the taking what is not given, **licentiousness**, and lying speech.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

> Slaughter of life, theft, lyings **adultery**: — To these no word of praise the wise award.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

> or (ili) **may be a fornicator, having intercourse with girls under the charge of mother or father or brother or sister or relations, yes, with girls affianced and plighted, and even wearing the very garlands of betrothal.**

MN41,Chalmers(1926),pp203–204

MN41 frames its examples with 'a man' as the agent. DN31's verse names 'adultery' and does not restrict who commits it. Applying the vice to a woman who joins in such a relation therefore follows from the verse's ungendered term. It is not a separate rule about who can commit the vice.

These are historical guardianship norms, attributed to the source. They supply no marriage or consent doctrine and no celibacy criterion.

## Lying speech

DN31 names lying. MN41 glosses it as deliberate false testimony, for self, others or trifling gain.

> The destruction of life, the taking what is not given, licentiousness, and **lying speech**.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

> a man (i) may be a liar ;—-when cited to give testimony before assembly or village-meeting or family council or royal household or his guild, **he may say that he knows when he does not know, or that he does not know when he does know, or that he saw when he did not see, or that he did not see when he did see,—deliberately lying in the interests either of himself or of other people or for some trifling gain.**

MN41,Chalmers(1926),pp203–204

The gloss turns on deliberate falsehood. An honest mistake is not shown by this source to be lying.

## Partiality

An evil deed done from partiality. It is not every preference.

> **Evil deeds are done from motives of partiality, enmity, stupidity and fear.** But inasmuch as the Ariyan disciple is not led away by these motives, he through them does no evil deed.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

The qualifier is an evil deed done from the motive. Fondness alone is not this vice.

DN31 gives the term without further gloss. Do not supply a detailed theory of chanda from memory.

## Stupidity

An evil deed done from stupidity (moha, delusion): wronging someone because one has not understood the facts or the right course. It is not mere lack of knowledge or cleverness.

> Evil deeds are done from motives of partiality, enmity, stupidity and fear. But inasmuch as the Ariyan disciple is not led away by these motives, he through them does no evil deed. … Whoso from partiality or hate Or fear or dulness doth transgress the Norm, All minished good name and fame become As in the ebbing month the waning moon.

Dīgha Nikāya 31 §§5–6, trans. Rhys Davids (1921), pp. 174–175

> Four ways of going astray, to wit, through partiality, hate, illusion, fear.

Dīgha Nikāya 33, fours xix, trans. Rhys Davids (1921)

The translator's note on the same four:

> Agatim gacchati, literally, he goes to a not-going, or wrong going, or impasse.

Rhys Davids' footnote to Dīgha Nikāya 33 (1921)

In the Kukkura Jātaka a king, enraged that his carriage straps were gnawed, gives the first order below; the Bodhisatta, born as a dog, later answers him:

> "Kill every dog you see." … "But, your majesty, if you do not know for certain the real culprits, it is not right to order the destruction of every dog that is seen." … "Therefore you are following … the four Evil Courses of partiality, dislike, ignorance and fear. Such courses are wrong, and not kinglike. For kings in trying cases should be as unbiassed as the beam of a balance."

Kukkura Jātaka (No. 22), trans. Chalmers, in Cowell (ed.), The Jātaka I (1895), pp. 59–60

The Pali motive is mohāgati, going wrong through delusion. Rhys Davids renders it 'stupidity', 'dulness' (in the verse) and 'illusion' (DN 33); Chalmers' Jātaka has 'ignorance'. The fault is the evil deed that delusion produces, not ignorance as such. The king's error was to condemn without knowing who was guilty. The Bodhisatta shows the contrary course: he reasons from the facts, proposes a test, and so prevents an unjust killing.

DN 31 names the motive without defining it; this guide leans on the Jātaka's application and on the translators' renderings of the same term. The Jātaka joins all four courses in one judgement, so it does not isolate stupidity from the others. The sources set no standard of care for honest mistakes.

## The habit of idleness

A habit of excuses that leaves work undone and wealth dwindling.

> Six, young householder, are the perils of the habit of idleness : — he says, it is too cold, and does no work. He says, it is too hot, and does no work ; ... **And while all that he should do remains undone, new wealth he does not get, and such wealth as he has dwindles away.**

DN31§§3–13,tr.TW/CAF Rhys Davids,DialoguesIII(1921),pp174–176

<summary>NIta - Some evidence that the conduct shows this vice.</summary>

Selected act or disposition (quoted from the passage):
So he entered it to get pleasure from it, paid scant attention to his exercise, ate his fill, spent the day in idle talk, and slept all night long.

Full passage:
Reborn in this Buddha-age at SavatthI as the son of a brahmin, he [NIta] was called NIta. When grown up he thought: ‘These Sakiyan recluses are very lucky in that they are well provided with all necessaries. It is a happy life, that of a member of the Order.’ So he entered it to get pleasure from it, paid scant attention to his exercise, ate his fill, spent the day in idle talk, and slept all night long. But the Master discerned the ripeness of his antecedents, and gave him this verse in admonition:

Thou all the night to slumber given o’er,
Who lov’st the day ’mid chattering crowds to spend: —
Dost deem that thou this way at any time,
Poor silly fool, of Ill shalt make an end?

Agitated by the Master’s words, he settled to develop insight, and not long after attained arahantship. He then confessed anna in repeating this verse.

Forward: The source describes a habit in which what one should do 'remains undone'. The story states as fact that NIta paid little attention to his exercise and spent his days in idle talk and his nights in sleep. Inference: for a monk, the 'exercise' is the work he should be doing, so this is a settled pattern of leaving it undone. The source's example is excuse-making about householder work and wealth. NIta's case is a monk neglecting practice, and the story quotes no excuses. That shortfall is why I hold back some weight from the top level. Reverse: The core of the vice is a habit in which 'all that he should do remains undone'. NIta's exercise, the work his role requires, is habitually neglected, and the trait is a standing disposition, not a lapse. Two features of the source's own framing hold this below the most confident level. The text records no specific excuses ('too cold', 'too hot'). And the wealth-loss consequence is framed for a householder, while his loss is spiritual. That last point is an analogy. Strong evidence of the vice, split between 'some' and 'clear'.

Full passage:
And is the brahmin [Dhananjani] zealous and earnest ?—asked Sariputta further of the Almsman.

How could earnest zeal possibly dwell in Dhananjani? He uses the king to fleece the brahmins and householders, and uses them to fleece the king. Also, his pious wife who came of a pious stock is dead now ; and he has taken to himself another wife who is not pious and comes of no pious stock.

...

Said Sariputta:—-May I rest assured, Dhananjani, that zeal and earnestness are yours ?

How can that be, when I have to support my parents, my wife and family, and my slaves and serving folk; and have to entertain my acquaintances and friends, my kith and kin, and guests, and have also to provide for my kinsfolk dead and gone, and for the deities, and for the king,—not to speak of supporting myself in meat and drink?

...

What think you, Dhananjani? Which is the better man?—He that for the sake of his parents departs from righteousness and equity? Or he that for their sake walks in righteousness and equity ?

The latter. For, to walk in righteousness and equity is better than to depart therefrom.

Moreover, Dhananjani, there are other courses of action which are justified and righteous in themselves, whereby he can support his parents and yet avoid evil-doing and walk uprightly. Now, does the same reasoning apply to the support of wife and family, ... and everything else ?

It does, Sariputta.

Fleecing is dishonest activity, not excuse-based idleness from ; he actively provides. Different vice.

## The taking what is not given

DN31 names theft; the MN41 gloss describes thievish appropriation in village and jungle.

> What are the four vices of conduct that he has put away ? The destruction of life, **the taking what is not given**, licentiousness, and lying speech.

Dīgha Nikāya 31, §§3–13, trans. T. W. / C. A. F. Rhys Davids, Dialogues III (1921), pp. 174–176

> or (ii) **may take what is not his,—by appropriating to himself in thievish fashion the belongings of other people in village and jungle**

MN41,Chalmers(1926),pp203–204

The source does not discuss borrowing, debt or disputed ownership. Do not treat those as theft without thievish appropriation.
